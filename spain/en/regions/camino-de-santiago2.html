Backpacking the Camino de Santiago

[kah-MEE-noh deh sahn-tee-AH-goh]

Say “Camino de Santiago” to most 20-somethings and they’ll picture a raincoat, a walking stick, and someone’s very religious aunt. Fair enough — for twelve centuries, this was pilgrimage business, plain and simple. But walk even the last hundred kilometres of it today and you’ll find something closer to a moving hostel party: strangers becoming best friends over shared blisters, albergues that turn into impromptu dinner parties every single night, and a finish-line atmosphere in Santiago’s cathedral square that’s less “spiritual awakening” and more “we survived this together, let’s go celebrate.”

It’s one of the best low-cost, high-connection weeks you can have backpacking Spain — no solemnity required.

Here’s the part most people don’t know: you don’t need eight hundred kilometres and six weeks off work to do this properly. To earn the official Compostela certificate, you only need to walk the final 100km into Santiago — which works out to almost exactly one week of comfortable daily walking.

That makes the Camino one of the most achievable big adventures on any backpacking Spain itinerary, and there are three genuinely different one-week routes to choose from: the Camino Francés from Sarria, the Camino Inglés from Ferrol, and the Camino Portugués from Tui.

Budget-wise, this might be the single cheapest week you’ll spend backpacking Spain. Pilgrim hostels cost as little as €8–€15 a night, pilgrim menus are a bargain, and once you’ve paid for your flights and the walk itself there’s genuinely very little left to spend money on. Bring good socks, a sense of humour, and a willingness to talk to strangers — everything else sorts itself out along the way. Oh, and bring earplugs: you’ll be sleeping in dorms inhabited by chronic snorers, and won’t get a decent night’s sleep without them.

Best route for first-timers: Camino Francés from Sarria

Quietest one-week route: Camino Inglés from Ferrol

Easiest terrain: Camino Portugués from Tui

Minimum distance for a Compostela: 100km on foot

Typical albergue price: €8–€15 per night

Most important item: Earplugs

What Is the Camino, Exactly?

The Camino de Santiago isn’t a single trail — it’s a whole network of historic pilgrim routes from all over Europe, all converging on Santiago de Compostela in Galicia, where tradition holds that the remains of the Apostle James are buried. It’s a UNESCO World Heritage route and, alongside things like the Appalachian Trail, genuinely one of the great long-distance walks of the world.

To receive the official Compostela certificate at the end, you need to walk at least the final 100km (or cycle the final 200km), getting your pilgrim passport stamped at least twice a day along that stretch as proof.

A Short History: From Medieval Superhighway to Backpacker Bucket List

The Camino’s origins trace to the early 9th century, when the tomb of the Apostle James was said to have been discovered in what became Santiago de Compostela, instantly making it one of Christendom’s three great pilgrimage destinations alongside Jerusalem and Rome. Pilgrim numbers boomed through the Middle Ages, then declined for centuries — by the 1980s, barely a few hundred people a year were completing the route.

What changed it was a deliberate revival effort by Galician authorities from the late 20th century onward, followed by a genuine pop-culture boost: the 2010 film The Way, starring Martin Sheen, is widely credited with a huge surge in English-speaking and backpacker interest. Today, hundreds of thousands of people walk some portion of the Camino every year, and the demographic has shifted hard toward secular, adventure-motivated younger travellers rather than traditional religious pilgrims.

The Language You’ll Hear

Galician and Spanish dominate along all three routes, with English working well as an informal common language among fellow walkers, who come from genuinely everywhere. You don’t need more than basic Spanish to get by — albergue hosts and café owners along the way deal with international pilgrims constantly.

Which Camino Should You Choose?

All three one-week routes get you to Santiago and qualify for a Compostela. The right one depends less on distance and more on what kind of week you want.

Camino Francés from Sarria

The classic choice, and by far the busiest — this is the final stretch of the most famous Camino route, starting in the Galician town of Sarria. Well-marked, dense with albergues, and genuinely the most social option: expect a rotating cast of the same faces at breakfast, lunch, and dinner for a week straight. Book albergue beds ahead in peak season, as this stretch fills up fast.

Camino Inglés from Ferrol

Historically the route walked by English and Irish pilgrims who arrived by ship at the Galician coast — genuinely quieter than the Francés, with a smaller, tighter-knit community of walkers and easier last-minute albergue availability, while still delivering the full week-long distance and the same finish in Santiago’s cathedral square.

Camino Portugués from Tui

The second most popular Camino overall, picked up here at Tui, right on the Portuguese border. Generally flatter and easier underfoot than the Francés, with a good but noticeably calmer social scene, and the novelty of technically starting your trip in a different country.

The Camino Nobody Tells You About

Most Camino guides explain the route. Few explain what actually happens when you put a few hundred tired, blistered, slightly sunburnt strangers into shared dorms, communal kitchens, and small-town bars for a week.

The 6am Fresh Air Crisis

At some point, usually before sunrise, someone will open the dorm window because they believe fresh air is medically essential. It may be freezing. It may be raining. It may be 5:47am. None of this will matter. You will lie there, wrapped in a sleeping-bag liner, listening to the sound of another person’s deeply held beliefs about ventilation.

The Laundry Hierarchy

By day three, you will know exactly who has washed their socks, who has not, and whose drying underwear has claimed the only sunny patch of railing outside the albergue. The Camino does not create hierarchy through wealth or status. It creates it through laundry.

The 5am Backpack Rustle

Someone will decide that 5am is an appropriate time to pack. They will unzip a bag with the delicacy of a demolition crew. They will drop a water bottle. They will turn on a headtorch. They will then whisper, “Sorry,” in a voice loud enough to wake the entire dorm.

The Camino Family

You will meet people at breakfast, walk with them for an hour, see them again at lunch, and somehow be sharing a table, a bottle of wine, and deeply personal life stories by dinner. By day four, they are your Camino family. By day seven, you will be emotionally unprepared to say goodbye.

The Post-Camino Comedown

Santiago is not the end. It is the beginning of a strange, slightly melancholy period in which you return home, unpack, and discover that nobody at work wants to hear about your feet, your blisters, or the man who opened the dorm window at 6am.

Choose a Camino by Mood

You do not choose a Camino just by distance. You choose it by the kind of week you want to have.

  • If you want the classic social Camino: walk the Camino Francés from Sarria.
  • If you want fewer crowds but the same certificate: walk the Camino Inglés from Ferrol.
  • If you want an easier, flatter week: walk the Camino Portugués from Tui.
  • If you want a quieter, more rugged route: consider the Camino Primitivo.
  • If you want sea views and a coastal feel: consider the Camino Portugués Coastal route.
  • If you want a longer, more demanding pilgrimage: consider the Camino del Norte.
  • If you want to walk until you run out of holiday: do the full Camino Francés — but only if you have the time, feet, and emotional resilience for it.

How to Survive a Camino Dorm

Albergues are cheap, social, and occasionally testing. You will share a room with strangers, a bathroom with everyone, and a sleeping schedule with people who believe 5:30am is a reasonable time to begin the day.

The essentials

  • Earplugs: non-negotiable.
  • Eye mask: equally non-negotiable.
  • Flip-flops: for showers, corridors, and avoiding whatever is happening on the bathroom floor.
  • Quick-dry towel: smaller, lighter, and less likely to remain damp for three days.
  • Headtorch: for early departures and midnight bathroom trips.
  • Lock: for your bag and valuables.
  • Power bank: because outlets are not always where you need them.

The unwritten rules

  • Pack the night before if you have an early start.
  • Use a headtorch, not the main light.
  • Do not put your backpack on the bed.
  • Do not leave wet clothes drying in communal spaces overnight.
  • Do not treat the kitchen as your personal restaurant.
  • Do not have a phone conversation at full volume beside someone sleeping.
  • Do not open the window without checking whether anyone is sleeping beneath it.

Augustus’s rule: if you must open the window at 6am, you must also accept responsibility for the emotional consequences.

What Your Week on the Camino Actually Looks Like

The Camino runs on a simple daily rhythm, whichever route you pick: up early, walk 15–25km following the yellow arrows and scallop-shell markers, stop constantly for coffee and pastries in tiny villages, arrive at your albergue by mid-afternoon, shower, nap, find dinner and your fellow walkers, sleep, repeat.

The emotional arc of a one-week Camino

  • Day 1: excitement, optimism, and a complete misunderstanding of what 20km feels like.
  • Day 2: blisters, doubts, and the first serious consideration of a taxi.
  • Day 3: your body adjusts, your Camino family forms, and everything becomes strangely enjoyable.
  • Day 4: you stop caring about your hair, your phone, and most of your original plans.
  • Day 5: you start talking about coming back before you have even finished.
  • Day 6: Santiago feels close, but you are not ready for it to end.
  • Day 7: you arrive, cry a little, drink something cold, and begin planning the next one.

The Waymarking

You genuinely cannot get lost — yellow arrows and scallop-shell symbols mark the entire route, painted on walls, posts, and pavements every few hundred metres. It’s one of the best-signposted long-distance trails in the world.

Getting Your Credencial Stamped

Collect at least two stamps a day from cafés, churches, town halls, and albergues along the way — it’s part practical requirement for your certificate, part genuinely fun scavenger-hunt element that gives the week a game-like structure.

Albergues, Hostels & Dorm Life

An albergue is a pilgrim-specific hostel: cheap communal-bunk accommodation, often €8–€15 a night, found roughly every few kilometres along each route. It is also where the Camino’s social life happens.

Types of accommodation

  • Municipal albergues: usually the cheapest, often first-come-first-served, and often the most basic.
  • Parish and religious albergues: sometimes cheaper and quieter; some have separate men’s and women’s rooms.
  • Private albergues: usually easier to book, with more facilities, but often more expensive.
  • Hostels and pensions: a good fallback if albergues are full or you need a private room.

Female-only dorms

Most Camino dorms are mixed, but some religious albergues and private hostels offer female-only rooms. If that matters to you, check the individual albergue before you set out — not after you have already arrived and discovered that your dorm contains a man who snores like a tractor with a grudge.

Snore Score

Snore Score: Our completely unscientific but emotionally accurate rating of how likely you are to get a decent night’s sleep. It considers dorm size, albergue style, nightlife, weather, and the Fresh Air Factor.

Augustus’s advice: bring earplugs, choose a lower bunk, and never sleep directly beneath the window. The Camino teaches many things, but it does not teach you how to win an argument with someone who has already decided that fresh air is more important than your frostbite.

What Happens After the Camino?

You arrive in Santiago, collect your Compostela, celebrate with people you met four days ago, and assume the hard part is over. It is not. The hard part is going home.

The post-Camino blues

For a week, your main decisions are: how far to walk, where to eat, and whether to have another glass of wine. Then you return to emails, commuting, and people who do not understand why you are emotionally attached to a blister plaster.

How to soften the landing

  • Stay an extra night or two in Santiago rather than flying home immediately.
  • Keep in touch with your Camino family, but accept that some friendships were perfect for the Camino and not necessarily for everyday life.
  • Do something physical in the first week back.
  • Plan a smaller walking trip or weekend hike.
  • Do not immediately book another Camino — unless you want to, in which case Augustus fully supports the decision.

Augustus’s advice: the Camino does not end in Santiago. It ends three weeks later, when you stop telling strangers about your feet.

Can You Do the Camino in a Wheelchair?

Yes — but not every route, stage, or albergue is equally accessible. The Camino is increasingly open to pilgrims with limited mobility, but planning matters much more than it does for an able-bodied walker.

In 2025, 257 wheelchair users received a Compostela in Santiago. That is proof that the Camino is not exclusively for people with good knees, expensive boots, and an unhealthy relationship with early mornings.

  • Choose a route and stages carefully; some paths, hills, and rural stretches are much harder than others.
  • Check accommodation accessibility directly before booking.
  • Consider luggage transfers and support services.
  • Plan shorter daily distances and build in rest days.
  • Do not assume that “accessible” means the same thing to every albergue.

Augustus’s advice: the Camino does not belong to one type of body, one pace, or one idea of what a pilgrimage should look like.

The Camino Regret Index

Everyone arrives with a packing list. The Camino quickly tells you which items were a mistake.

  • Regret bringing: a second pair of shoes, a heavy book, a full-size towel, a sleeping bag you do not need, and any item you describe as “just in case”.
  • Regret not bringing: proper blister plasters, a spare pair of socks, earplugs, a power bank, and a small amount of humility.
  • Regret doing: walking too far on day one, skipping lunch, and believing that a 30km day will “save time”.
  • Regret not doing: stopping for coffee, talking to strangers, and taking an extra day in Santiago.

Augustus’s rule: if you are carrying something “just in case”, you are carrying it for a version of yourself who does not exist.

The Quickest Camino

Augustus von Spaß presents: The Quickest Camino. The fastest known time for the Camino Francés is 6 days, 14 hours and 49 minutes. Augustus considers this impressive, insane, and a clear failure to appreciate the strategic importance of café con leche.

For normal humans, Augustus recommends the opposite challenge: The Slowest Possible Camino. Take five weeks. Stop for lunch. Talk to strangers. Get lost in a small town. Sleep badly in an albergue. Arrive in Santiago looking like you have actually been on holiday.

  • The Quickest Camino: complete the final 100km as fast as you safely can.
  • The One-Week Camino: walk Sarria to Santiago in seven days.
  • The Two-Week Camino: walk a longer section over 14 days.
  • The Slow Camino: take at least 30 days, with no more than 20km a day.
  • The Café con Leche Camino: stop for coffee at least once every 8km. Augustus’s preferred category.

The fastest known time is not a backpacking challenge. It is an ultra-endurance event. If you want to try it, train properly, plan your support and safety, and do not confuse a pilgrimage with a personality test.

Staying Safe & Travelling Well on the Camino

The Camino is about as safe as backpacking Spain gets — well-populated, well-marked, and full of fellow walkers and locals used to pilgrims passing through. The real hazards here have nothing to do with crime and everything to do with your feet, the weather, and simple overexertion.

Blisters Are the Real Enemy

Break in proper walking shoes or boots well before you go — never for the first time on day one. Merino wool socks, blister plasters, and stopping the moment you feel a hot spot will save your week more reliably than anything else on this list.

Pace Yourself

The most common rookie mistake is walking too far, too fast, on day one out of excitement, then paying for it for the rest of the week. Start conservatively and let your daily distance grow as your body adjusts.

Weather and Layering

All three routes pass through Galicia, one of the rainiest parts of Spain — pack a proper waterproof layer regardless of season, and sun protection for the clearer stretches too.

Road Crossings

The route occasionally crosses or briefly follows local roads — stay alert at these points, especially in low light or fog, since waymarking focuses on direction rather than traffic safety.

Albergue Belongings

Theft is rare but not unheard of in busy communal albergues — keep valuables on you or in a small lockable bag rather than left loose in a dorm.

Free Camino

  • Village water fountains: free, safe drinking water at regular intervals along all three routes.
  • Credencial stamps: every stamp along the way is free.
  • Santiago’s cathedral and old town: free to enter and wander.
  • The Pilgrim’s Mass: free to attend, and one of the most atmospheric things you can do in Santiago.

Nightlife: How It Actually Works

Along the route itself, “nightlife” mostly means a long dinner, a few glasses of local wine, and an early night before another day of walking — and that is genuinely part of the appeal.

In Santiago, once you have finished, it is a different story entirely. Bars around the old town fill with freshly arrived pilgrims still in their walking gear, buzzing off the achievement, and it is completely normal to end up celebrating with people you only met three days earlier on the trail.

The Big Hits

Your First View of the Cathedral

Nothing beats the moment the cathedral’s spires first come into view — genuinely emotional, whatever your reasons for walking.

Praza do Obradoiro

The grand square where every route ends, directly in front of the cathedral. Sit down, take your boots off, and watch the next wave of arriving pilgrims go through exactly what you just did.

The Pilgrim’s Mass & the Botafumeiro

Held daily inside the cathedral, and on special occasions featuring the botafumeiro — an enormous incense burner swung by ropes the full length of the transept by a team of handlers.

The Pilgrim’s Office

Where you’ll present your stamped credencial and collect your Compostela certificate — a small, satisfying piece of admin that caps off the whole week.

Camino FAQs for Backpackers

Do I really only need to walk 100km?

Yes — 100km on foot, or 200km by bike, is the official minimum distance to receive the Compostela certificate. All three one-week routes above are built around that distance.

Which route should I pick?

Camino Francés from Sarria if you want the classic experience and the biggest social scene. Camino Inglés from Ferrol if you would rather avoid the crowds. Camino Portugués from Tui if flatter terrain and a slightly different flavour appeal to you.

How fit do I need to be?

You do not need to be an athlete. Most people cover 15–25km a day at a comfortable walking pace. Break in your footwear before you go; that matters more than raw fitness.

What is an albergue, and how do I book one?

An albergue is a pilgrim-specific hostel — cheap communal-bunk accommodation, often €8–€15 a night. On the busy Camino Francés in peak season, many are first-come-first-served, so arriving by early-to-mid afternoon matters. On quieter routes, booking a day or two ahead is usually enough.

How do I get my pilgrim passport and certificate?

Buy your credencial at your starting point, get it stamped at least twice a day for the final 100km, then present it at the Pilgrim’s Office in Santiago to collect your free Compostela.

Do I need to be religious?

Not remotely. Most walkers today are doing it for the challenge, the scenery, and the social experience rather than religious reasons.

When is the best time to go?

May, June, and September give you good weather without July and August’s peak crowds and albergue competition.

Is the tap water safe?

Yes. Refill at village fountains and cafés along the way rather than carrying more than you need.

Can I wild camp along the Camino?

Not legally. Galicia prohibits camping outside authorised campsites. Albergues are cheap, plentiful, and part of the experience; if you want to carry a tent anyway, use an authorised campsite.
