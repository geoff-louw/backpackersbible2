Backpacking Asturias

[ahs-TOO-ryahs]

There's an old Spanish saying — quien no ha visto Asturias, no ha visto España, "whoever hasn't seen Asturias hasn't seen Spain" — and it's not just regional pride talking. Officially, the region brands itself Asturias, Paraíso Natural (Natural Paradise), and for once a tourism slogan actually undersells it: a coastline of hidden coves and towering sea cliffs, the Picos de Europa mountain range delivering some of the best hiking in the country, cider poured from shoulder height as a genuine local sport, and a version of Spain that looks, tastes, and moves nothing like the postcards.

For backpackers coming off the sun-and-sangria stretch of a trip, Asturias is a proper reset — part of the "Green Spain" belt across the north, with rolling hills, real mountains, and a climate that swaps beach weather for hiking weather. It's genuinely underrated on any backpacking Spain route: fewer international tourists than Galicia or the Basque Country, but just as much natural drama, plus a cider culture (sidra, poured with real ceremony from above the head) that turns even an ordinary Tuesday night out into an event.

On cost, Asturias is one of the best-value regions left for backpacking — cheaper than the Basque Country, with hostels, cider, and food all priced kindly. Combine that with genuinely spectacular scenery and a laid-back, unhurried local pace, and it's an easy region to fall behind schedule in, in the best possible way.

What Kind of Place Is This, Exactly?

Asturias sits on Spain's north coast, wedged between Galicia to the west and Cantabria to the east, with the Picos de Europa mountain range rising sharply along its southern border with Castile and León. It holds a genuinely unique constitutional distinction: Asturias is the only Spanish region ever to carry the title "Principality," and to this day the heir to the Spanish throne is formally titled the Prince or Princess of Asturias.

A Short History: Where the Reconquista Actually Began

Asturias holds a genuinely outsized place in Spanish history: in 718 AD, at the Battle of Covadonga, a small Christian force under Pelagius (Don Pelayo) defeated a Moorish army in these mountains, founding the Kingdom of Asturias — the first Christian kingdom to resist the Muslim conquest of Iberia, and traditionally marked as the starting point of the entire, nearly 800-year Reconquista that shaped the rest of Spanish history. Centuries later, the region built a different identity around its 19th- and 20th-century coal and steel industry, giving it a grittier, more industrial character in places than the purely agricultural image "Green Spain" might suggest.

The Language You'll Hear

Asturian (locally called Bable or Asturianu) is a genuine distinct Romance language, though — unlike Catalan, Basque, or Galician — it holds only "protected" status rather than full official recognition, a point of ongoing local debate. Castilian Spanish dominates daily life in the cities; Asturian is more commonly heard in rural areas and among older generations.

Understanding Asturias: The Zones

Oviedo

The regional capital, home to a set of extraordinary 9th-century pre-Romanesque churches (UNESCO-listed, and unlike anything else in Spain) and Calle Gascona — the city's "cider boulevard," lined end to end with sidrerías.

Gijón

A coastal city with a proper urban beach, a livelier and younger nightlife scene than Oviedo, and a good base if you want the mountains and the sea both within easy reach.

The Picos de Europa

A dramatic limestone mountain range shared with Cantabria and Castile y León, but most accessibly explored from the Asturian side — glacial lakes, the famous Cares Gorge trail, and some of the best hiking anywhere in Spain.

The Coast: Cudillero & Llanes

A string of dramatic fishing villages and hidden beaches, including the genuinely bizarre Playa de Gulpiyuri — a full sandy beach sitting inland, fed by the sea through an underground tunnel.

Cider Country

The rural apple-orchard interior, dotted with working llagares (cider houses) that open their barrels to visitors, especially in autumn during pressing season.

Asturias FAQs for Backpackers

When is the best time to go?

June to September gives you the best hiking weather, though — as with the rest of Green Spain — pack for rain regardless of your dates. Autumn brings cider-pressing season, a genuinely fun time to visit if the harvest side of things interests you.

Should I base myself in Oviedo or Gijón?

Oviedo for cider culture, pre-Romanesque architecture, and easier access inland to the Picos. Gijón for a beach-and-city combination and a slightly livelier nightlife scene. Both are small enough to combine easily.

How does cider pouring (escanciar) actually work?

Asturian cider is poured from high above the head into a wide glass held low, aerating the flat cider as it falls — you're meant to drink it in one go before it goes flat again, then pass the glass on. Don't worry about doing it perfectly yourself; most bars pour for you, and watching the ritual is half the fun.

Do I need to speak Spanish or Asturian?

Spanish covers you everywhere. English is spoken less widely here than in Barcelona or Madrid, so a little Spanish helps, especially outside Oviedo and Gijón.

How do I get around?

Buses and trains connect Oviedo and Gijón easily. The Picos de Europa and the coastal villages are best explored by bus (services thin out but exist) or, ideally, a rental car.

Is the tap water safe?

Yes, throughout Asturias. Perfectly safe to drink.

Is Asturias safe?

Yes — genuinely one of the safest, lowest-crime regions in Spain. See our full safety section below for the one real consideration: the mountains.

Staying Safe & Travelling Well in Asturias

Asturias is a low-crime, low-hassle region for backpackers, with no pickpocketing reputation to speak of. The genuine safety considerations here are about terrain and weather, not people.

Mountain Weather Changes Fast

The Picos de Europa can shift from clear skies to fog and rain within an hour, even in summer. Check forecasts before any serious hike, carry proper layers, and don't push on into deteriorating conditions on exposed ridgelines.

Coastal Safety

The Atlantic coastline here has real currents and swell, particularly at less-supervised beaches like Gulpiyuri and the smaller coves near Cudillero — check conditions before swimming anywhere without a lifeguard.

Weather and Layering

As with the rest of Green Spain, rain is a genuine year-round possibility. Bring a proper waterproof layer whatever your dates.

Petty Theft

A minimal issue here compared to Spain's bigger cities, though standard precautions are still sensible in Oviedo and Gijón's busier bars during festival weekends.

Quick Facts

Best Time To Visit:
June to September for the best hiking weather — pack for rain regardless, and consider autumn for cider-pressing season.
Best For:
Mountains, hidden beaches, cider culture, and a genuinely underrated stretch of Green Spain.
Most Popular Experience:
Hiking the Cares Gorge trail and the Covadonga Lakes in the Picos de Europa.
Most Under-rated Experience:
Playa de Gulpiyuri — a full sandy beach sitting inland, fed by the sea through an underground tunnel.
Best Free Experiences:
Oviedo's pre-Romanesque churches, Gijón's city beach, and wandering any of the coastal fishing villages.
Ideal Stay:
5–7 days to cover Oviedo, Gijón, and at least one proper day in the Picos de Europa.
Budget Level:
One of the best-value regions left in Spain. Expect roughly €35–€70 per day.
Getting Around:
Buses and trains connect Oviedo and Gijón easily; a rental car is the best way to properly explore the Picos and the coast.
Best Photo Spots:
The Covadonga Lakes at sunrise, the Cares Gorge trail, Gulpiyuri's inland beach, and Cudillero's cliffside houses.

1. The Picos de Europa (Non-Negotiable)

This mountain range is the single biggest reason to come to Asturias, and it delivers on every level — glacial lakes, dramatic gorges, and hiking that rivals anywhere in the Alps.

The Covadonga Lakes

Two glacial lakes, Enol and Ercina, sitting at the historic heart of Spain's first national park, right beside the site of the 718 AD battle that founded the Kingdom of Asturias. Drive up yourself (arrive early — access is restricted and shuttle-bus-only in peak season) or take a guided day tour from Oviedo. Guided tours from roughly €40.

The Cares Gorge Trail (Ruta del Cares)

A spectacular, mostly flat 12km trail carved into a limestone gorge, with a path blasted directly into the cliff face — genuinely one of the best day hikes in Spain, and free to walk.

Read More

Fuente Dé Cable Car

A short cable car ride up to 1,800m, opening up high-altitude hiking without the climb — a good option if you're short on time but still want the big mountain views.

2. Adrenaline

Whitewater rafting and canoeing the Sella River: The Sella is genuinely famous for this — canoeing and rafting trips run through the Picos foothills, and the river hosts the Descenso Internacional del Sella, a major annual canoe race that turns into a full festival (see Live Sport below).

Via ferrata in the Picos de Europa: Several protected climbing routes let you scramble sections of the mountains with cables and ladders, no technical climbing experience required.

Surfing the Asturian coast: A real, growing surf scene along the north coast, with beginner-friendly breaks and board hire widely available near Gijón.

3. The Coast: Hidden Beaches & Fishing Villages

Most backpackers touring Spain never make it this far north — which means Asturias's coastline stays genuinely uncrowded even in summer.

Playa de Gulpiyuri

A full sandy beach sitting entirely inland, a few hundred metres from the sea, fed by an underground tunnel that floods it with tide water — one of the strangest, most photogenic natural features in Spain.

Cudillero & Llanes

Cudillero is a working fishing village stacked dramatically up a cliffside; Llanes has a proper old town, good beaches, and easy access to the Picos. Both reward a slow wander rather than a rushed visit.

4. Free Asturias (Zero Euros)

Oviedo's pre-Romanesque churches: The exteriors of these extraordinary 9th-century buildings cost nothing to admire, even if you skip the paid interior visits.

Gijón's city beach: Free, public, and a proper stretch of sand right in the middle of a major city.

The Cares Gorge trail: Already covered above, and free to walk in full.

Any coastal village: Cudillero, Llanes, and the smaller fishing villages between them cost nothing to wander and are some of the best free experiences in the region.

5. Nightlife: How It Actually Works

In Asturias, nightlife starts with cider, not cocktails — a proper night out here means moving between sidrerías, drinking escanciado cider poured from above the head, and grazing on cheap tapas as you go.

For a first drink

Oviedo's Calle Gascona — the "cider boulevard" — is lined end to end with sidrerías and built for exactly this kind of bar-hopping evening.

For clubbing

Gijón has the livelier, younger late-night scene of the two main cities, particularly around its beachfront bars in summer.

For something more local

Head into any rural llagar (cider house) during pressing season for a genuinely local night out, far removed from anything built for tourists.

6. The Big Hits (Things You Must Not Skip)

The Covadonga Lakes

Already covered above, but it bears repeating — the single essential natural sight in the entire region.

The Cares Gorge Trail

Covered in full above — one of the best day hikes in the country.

Oviedo's Old Town & Pre-Romanesque Churches

A compact, walkable old town built around genuinely unique 9th-century architecture found nowhere else in Spain.

Playa de Gulpiyuri

Covered in full above — one of the strangest, most photogenic natural quirks anywhere on the Spanish coast.

7. Live Sport & Culture

Real Sporting de Gijón: The region's biggest football club, with a passionate local following — a genuinely good, comparatively affordable way to catch live Spanish football depending on the division that season.

The Descenso Internacional del Sella: A major annual canoe race down the Sella River every August, which has grown into one of the wildest, most anticipated party weekends in the region — thousands of spectators line the riverbanks, and the towns of Arriondas and Ribadesella turn into a full festival for the day.

Budget Accommodation In Asturias

Hostels listed on Booking.com and Hostelworld

Booking.com HOSTELWORLD

A quick note about Spanish accommodation: a hostel, hostal and albergue are not necessarily the same thing. Hostels are the familiar backpacker option, while a hostal is usually a cheap private guesthouse. Albergue can mean a youth hostel, municipal hostel, mountain refuge or Camino pilgrim hostel, each with different rules. Regional languages can add to the confusion too — for example, Catalonia uses alberg and the Basque Country uses aterpetxe. There are also networks such as REAJ, Hostelling International and Catalonia's Xanascat.
