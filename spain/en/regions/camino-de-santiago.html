The Camino

Backpacking the Camino de Santiago (The Last 100km)

Say "Camino de Santiago" to most 20-somethings and picture a raincoat, a walking stick, and someone's very religious aunt. Fair enough — for twelve centuries, this was pilgrimage business, plain and simple. But walk even the last hundred kilometres of it today and you'll find something closer to a moving hostel party: strangers becoming best friends over shared blisters, albergues that turn into impromptu dinner parties every single night, and a finish-line atmosphere in Santiago's cathedral square that's less "spiritual awakening" and more "we survived this together, let's go celebrate." It's one of the best low-cost, high-connection weeks you can have backpacking Spain — no solemnity required.

Here's the part most people don't know: you don't need eight hundred kilometres and six weeks off work to do this properly. To earn the official Compostela certificate, you only need to walk the final 100km into Santiago — which works out to almost exactly one week of comfortable daily walking. That makes the Camino one of the most achievable big adventures on any backpacking Spain itinerary, and there are three genuinely different one-week routes to choose from: the Camino Francés from Sarria (the classic, busiest, most social option), the Camino Inglés from Ferrol (shorter, quieter, historically the route English and Irish pilgrims walked after arriving by ship), and the Camino Portugués from Tui (flatter, second-most-popular, arriving up from the Portuguese border).

Budget-wise, this might be the single cheapest week you'll spend backpacking Spain. Pilgrim hostels (albergues) run from as little as €8–€15 a night, pilgrim menus are a bargain, and once you've paid for your flights and the walk itself there's genuinely very little left to spend money on. Bring good socks, a sense of humour, and a willingness to talk to strangers — everything else sorts itself out along the way.

What Is The Camino, Exactly?

The Camino de Santiago isn't a single trail — it's a whole network of historic pilgrim routes from all over Europe, all converging on Santiago de Compostela in Galicia, where tradition holds that the remains of the Apostle James are buried. It's a UNESCO World Heritage route and, alongside things like the Appalachian Trail, genuinely one of the great long-distance walks of the world. To receive the official Compostela certificate at the end, you need to walk at least the final 100km (or cycle the final 200km), getting your pilgrim passport (credencial) stamped at least twice a day along that stretch as proof.

A Short History: From Medieval Superhighway to Backpacker Bucket List

The Camino's origins trace to the early 9th century, when the tomb of the Apostle James was said to have been discovered in what became Santiago de Compostela, instantly making it one of Christendom's three great pilgrimage destinations alongside Jerusalem and Rome. Pilgrim numbers boomed through the Middle Ages, then declined for centuries — by the 1980s, barely a few hundred people a year were completing the route. What changed it was a deliberate revival effort by Galician authorities from the late 20th century onward, followed by a genuine pop-culture boost: the 2010 film The Way, starring Martin Sheen, is widely credited with a huge surge in English-speaking and backpacker interest. Today, hundreds of thousands of people walk some portion of the Camino every year, and the demographic has shifted hard toward secular, adventure-motivated younger travellers rather than traditional religious pilgrims.

The Language You'll Hear

Galician and Spanish dominate along all three routes, with English working well as an informal common language among fellow walkers, who come from genuinely everywhere. You don't need more than basic Spanish to get by — albergue hosts and cafe owners along the way deal with international pilgrims constantly.

Understanding Your Options: The Three One-Week Routes

Camino Francés from Sarria (approx. 110km)

The classic choice, and by far the busiest — this is the final stretch of the most famous Camino route, starting in the Galician town of Sarria. Well-marked, dense with albergues, and genuinely the most social option: expect a rotating cast of the same faces at breakfast, lunch, and dinner for a week straight. Book albergue beds ahead in peak season (July–August), as this stretch fills up fast.

Camino Inglés from Ferrol (approx. 118km)

Historically the route walked by English and Irish pilgrims who arrived by ship at the Galician coast — genuinely quieter than the Francés, with a smaller, tighter-knit community of walkers and easier last-minute albergue availability, while still delivering the full week-long distance and the same finish in Santiago's cathedral square.

Camino Portugués from Tui (approx. 115km)

The second most popular Camino overall, picked up here at Tui, right on the Portuguese border. Generally flatter and easier underfoot than the Francés, with a good but noticeably calmer social scene, and the novelty of technically starting your trip in a different country.

Camino FAQs for Backpackers

Do I really only need to walk 100km?

Yes — 100km on foot (or 200km by bike) is the official minimum distance to receive the Compostela certificate, and all three routes above are built around exactly that distance, worked out to roughly a week of walking at a comfortable pace.

Which route should I pick?

Camino Francés from Sarria if you want the classic experience and the biggest social scene. Camino Inglés from Ferrol if you'd rather avoid the crowds while still getting the full week. Camino Portugués from Tui if flatter terrain and a slightly different flavour appeal to you.

How fit do I need to be?

You don't need to be an athlete — most people cover 15–25km a day at a comfortable walking pace, and the terrain on all three routes is manageable for anyone in reasonably normal shape. Break in your footwear before you go; that matters more than raw fitness.

What's an albergue, and how do I book one?

An albergue is a pilgrim-specific hostel — cheap communal-bunk accommodation, often €8–€15 a night, found roughly every few kilometres along each route. On the busy Camino Francés in peak season, many are first-come-first-served, so arriving by early-to-mid afternoon matters; on the quieter Inglés and Portugués routes, and outside peak summer, booking a day or two ahead is usually enough.

How do I get my pilgrim passport and the certificate?

Buy your credencial (pilgrim passport) at your starting point — most albergues, tourist offices, and pilgrim associations sell them cheaply. Get it stamped at least twice a day for the final 100km, then present it at the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago to collect your free Compostela certificate (a paid, more detailed distance certificate is also available if you want it).

Do I need to be religious to do this?

Not remotely — most walkers today are doing it for the challenge, the scenery, and the social experience rather than religious reasons, and nobody will look twice at you for that. The Pilgrim's Mass in the cathedral is worth attending as a cultural experience either way.

When's the best time to go?

May, June, and September give you good weather without July and August's peak crowds and albergue competition. Spring and autumn also mean cooler daytime walking temperatures, which matters more than you'd think over several consecutive days on your feet.

Is the tap water safe?

Yes, throughout the route. Refill at village fountains and cafes along the way rather than carrying more than you need.

Staying Safe & Travelling Well on the Camino

The Camino is about as safe as backpacking Spain gets — well-populated, well-marked, and full of fellow walkers and locals used to pilgrims passing through. The real hazards here have nothing to do with crime and everything to do with your feet, the weather, and simple overexertion.

Blisters Are the Real Enemy

Break in proper walking shoes or boots well before you go — never for the first time on day one. Merino wool socks, blister plasters, and stopping the moment you feel a hot spot (not after it's already blistered) will save your week more reliably than anything else on this list.

Pace Yourself

The most common rookie mistake is walking too far, too fast, on day one out of excitement, then paying for it for the rest of the week. Start conservatively and let your daily distance grow as your body adjusts.

Weather and Layering

All three routes pass through Galicia, one of the rainiest parts of Spain — pack a proper waterproof layer regardless of season, and sun protection for the clearer stretches too.

Road Crossings

The route occasionally crosses or briefly follows local roads — stay alert at these points, especially in low light or fog, since waymarking focuses on direction rather than traffic safety.

Albergue Belongings

Theft is rare but not unheard of in busy communal albergues — keep valuables on you or in a small lockable bag rather than left loose in a dorm.

Quick Facts

Best Time To Visit:
May, June, and September for good weather without peak-season albergue competition on the Camino Francés.
Best For:
A genuine physical challenge, a certificate to show for it, and one of the most sociable weeks you'll have backpacking Spain.
Most Popular Experience:
Walking the Camino Francés from Sarria — the busiest, most social, most classic option.
Most Under-rated Experience:
The Camino Inglés from Ferrol — the same distance and certificate, with a fraction of the crowds.
Best Free Experiences:
Free stamps at town halls and churches, village water fountains, communal albergue dinners, and Santiago's cathedral square.
Ideal Stay:
Exactly 6–7 days for the 100km minimum, plus 1–2 extra in Santiago to celebrate and recover.
Budget Level:
One of the cheapest weeks in backpacking Spain. Expect roughly €25–€45 per day including albergues.
Getting Around:
Buses and trains reach Sarria, Ferrol, and Tui from Santiago or Madrid; after that, it's entirely on foot.
Best Photo Spots:
Your first glimpse of the cathedral spires from the road into Santiago, and the euphoria in Praza do Obradoiro at the finish.

What Your Week On The Camino Actually Looks Like

1. Walking (Yes, It's the Main Activity — Here's How It Actually Works)

The Camino runs on a simple daily rhythm, whichever route you pick: up early (often before sunrise, especially in summer to beat the heat), walk 15–25km following the yellow arrows and scallop-shell markers, stop constantly for coffee and pastries in tiny villages, arrive at your albergue by mid-afternoon, shower, nap, find dinner and your fellow walkers, sleep, repeat.

The Waymarking

You genuinely cannot get lost — yellow arrows and scallop-shell symbols mark the entire route, painted on walls, posts, and pavements every few hundred metres. It's one of the best-signposted long-distance trails in the world.

Getting Your Credencial Stamped

Collect at least two stamps (sellos) a day from cafes, churches, town halls, and albergues along the way — it's part practical requirement for your certificate, part genuinely fun scavenger-hunt element that gives the week a game-like structure.

2. Beyond Walking: Optional Extras

Extending to Finisterre or Muxía: Many walkers add three or four extra days after Santiago, continuing on foot to the dramatic Atlantic coast at Finisterre ("End of the World") or the smaller, quieter Muxía — historically considered the true end of the pilgrimage, long before Santiago itself took over that role.

Cycling instead of walking: All three routes are rideable, and the certificate threshold drops to 200km by bike — a genuinely good option if you want to cover more ground or you're short on time.

Coasteering near Ferrol (Camino Inglés only): If you're starting from the coast, a day added on either side for cliff-jumping and coastal scrambling near Ferrol is an easy adrenaline add-on before you start walking.

3. Albergue Culture & The Social Side

This is genuinely the best part of the whole experience, and the reason so many people describe the Camino as one of the best weeks of their year, not just a hike.

The Communal Dinner

Many albergues serve a set "pilgrim menu" (menú del peregrino) — three courses and wine for €10–€15, eaten at long shared tables with whoever else is staying that night. It's the single easiest way to make friends fast, and most people end up walking at least part of the following days with people they met over dinner.

The "Camino Family"

Because everyone's walking at roughly similar daily distances, you'll keep bumping into the same faces at breakfast, on the trail, and at dinner for days in a row — a genuine, if temporary, found family that a lot of people describe as the actual highlight of the trip, more than any single sight along the way.

4. Free Camino (Zero Euros)

Village water fountains: Free, safe drinking water at regular intervals along all three routes — no need to buy bottled water at any point.

Credencial stamps: Every stamp along the way is free, and collecting them costs nothing but a quick stop.

Santiago's cathedral and old town: Free to enter and wander, and worth the time regardless of how you arrived.

The Pilgrim's Mass: Free to attend, and one of the most atmospheric things you can do in Santiago, religious or not.

5. Nightlife: How It Actually Works

Along the route itself, "nightlife" mostly means a long dinner, a few glasses of local wine, and an early night before another day of walking — and that's genuinely part of the appeal. Santiago, once you've finished, is a different story entirely.

On the trail

Small-town bars along all three routes are used to pilgrims and often stay open a little later than you'd expect for a village that size — a good place for a low-key nightcap with your Camino family.

In Santiago, after you finish

The finish-line atmosphere in Santiago is genuinely something else — bars around the old town fill with freshly arrived pilgrims still in their walking gear, buzzing off the achievement, and it's completely normal to end up celebrating with people you only met three days earlier on the trail.

6. The Big Hits (Things You Must Not Skip)

Your First View of the Cathedral

Nothing on this list beats the moment the cathedral's spires first come into view — genuinely emotional, whatever your reasons for walking, and impossible to properly describe until it happens to you.

Praza do Obradoiro

The grand square where every route ends, directly in front of the cathedral — sit down, take your boots off, and watch the next wave of arriving pilgrims go through exactly what you just did.

The Pilgrim's Mass & The Botafumeiro

Held daily inside the cathedral, and on special occasions featuring the botafumeiro — an enormous incense burner swung by ropes the full length of the transept by a team of handlers. Genuinely spectacular if your visit lines up with it.

The Pilgrim's Office

Where you'll present your stamped credencial and collect your Compostela certificate — a small, satisfying piece of admin that caps off the whole week.

7. Live Sport & Culture

Celta Vigo: If you're walking the Camino Portugués through the Vigo area, keep an eye out for a home match — a passionate, comparatively affordable La Liga matchday experience if the timing lines up.

Local fiestas along the route: Small-town festivals, complete with fireworks, live music, and communal meals, pop up along all three routes through the warmer months — genuinely one of the best surprises of walking through rural Galicia rather than just visiting its cities.
