Backpacking Ceuta & Melilla

[THEH-oo-tah] & [meh-LEE-yah]

Here's a quiz question: which parts of Spain are in Africa? The answer is two small cities, Ceuta and Melilla, on the north coast of Morocco. They fly the Spanish flag, use the euro and speak Spanish. But step outside the old walls and you're looking at Morocco. Think Moorish forts, Modernist mansions, mint tea next to tapas, and church bells mixing with the call to prayer. Few backpackers ever come, so you'll meet locals instead of crowds.

These are small places, so each takes a day or two to explore. That makes them a great add-on to an Andalusia trip, or a stepping stone into Morocco. Ceuta faces Gibraltar across the Strait, about an hour from Algeciras by ferry. Melilla sits around 400 km further east, and you reach it by overnight ferry or a short flight. Both feel different from the rest of Spain, and that's the fun of it. Both cities have also been in the news this year, so please read our safety section before you book.

For budget travel, these cities are a mixed bag. Fuel, drinks and electronics are cheap, because the cities have lower taxes than the rest of Spain. But proper hostels are rare, so expect to stay in simple budget hotels or hostales. Book ahead, especially in summer and during local fiestas. Many backpackers stay for a night or two, then carry on to Andalusia or cross into Morocco.

What Kind of Place Is This, Exactly?

Ceuta and Melilla are two "autonomous cities" of Spain, on the northern tip of Africa. Each has about 85,000 people. They are part of Spain, the EU and the Schengen travel area, but they have lower taxes than the mainland. Their land borders with Morocco are the only ones between the EU and Africa. That's why you'll see tall fences, border police and lots of security. Morocco claims both cities, and Spain says they are Spanish and not up for negotiation.

A Short History: Pillars, Portuguese and a Civil War

The Phoenicians were here first, and the Romans named Ceuta after the "seven brothers", the seven hills around it. Legend says Monte Hacho, above Ceuta, is one of the Pillars of Hercules, with the Rock of Gibraltar as the other. Portugal captured Ceuta in 1415, and it passed to Spain in the 1500s. Spain took Melilla in 1497. In 1936, army officers in Melilla began the revolt that led to the Spanish Civil War. Both cities then served as bases during Spain's rule over northern Morocco, which ended in 1956.

The Language You'll Hear

Spanish is the main language, but you'll also hear Moroccan Arabic, called Darija, all around you. In Melilla you may hear Tamazight, a Berber language. English isn't widely spoken, especially outside the hotels. A few Spanish words will help, and so will "salam", the Arabic greeting, which locals appreciate.

Four Cultures, One City

Both cities have large Christian and Muslim communities, plus smaller Jewish and Hindu ones. It makes for a very mixed feel. You'll find a church on one corner and a mosque on the next, tapas bars beside tea houses, and a food scene that mixes Spain with Morocco. Everyone mostly gets along, and it's one of the best things about the place.

Understanding the Cities & Zones

Ceuta

A busy port city on a narrow stretch of land, with the sea on two sides. It has royal walls with a water-filled moat, the hilltop fort on Monte Hacho and a lively old centre around Plaza de África. Town beaches and seafront promenades line the coast. Ferries run from Algeciras in about an hour.

Melilla

A calmer, neater city, with a huge old fortress called Melilla la Vieja on a rocky headland. Its centre is full of Modernist buildings from the early 1900s, with curved balconies and bright tiles. Wide streets, parks and beaches make it a pleasant place to wander.

The Moroccan Side

Many backpackers use these cities as a gateway to Morocco. From Ceuta, the Moroccan towns of Tetouan and Chefchaouen, the "blue city", are close by. From Melilla, the nearest big town is Nador. The land crossings can close at short notice, so check the latest news before you plan a day trip.

How Spain Differs From Home

Spain runs on a late clock, and Ceuta and Melilla follow it. Lunch is at 2pm, and dinner starts at 9pm or later. Many shops close in the afternoon, and on Sundays most are shut. Because of the large Muslim communities, some cafés and areas are quiet or don't serve alcohol, and Ramadan changes the rhythm of the day. Evenings come alive after sunset. Dress is relaxed, but cover up a bit when you visit mosques or religious sites.

Ceuta & Melilla FAQs for Backpackers

When is the best time to go?

Spring (April to June) and autumn (September to October) are best, with warm days and fewer crowds. Summer is hot and sticky, and winter is mild but often wet. In Ceuta, a wind from the east called the levante can bring cloud and fog for days. Local fiestas fill up the cities in late summer, so book early.

Is it really Spain?

Yes. Ceuta and Melilla are Spanish cities in the EU and the Schengen area. You pay in euros, and the rules are Spanish. One difference is that everyone is checked when leaving for the mainland. Police check ID at the port and the airport, so always carry your passport, even though the journey is within Spain.

How do I get there?

For Ceuta, take a ferry from Algeciras, which takes about an hour. For Melilla, take a long ferry from Málaga or Almería, or a short flight from Málaga, Almería, Madrid or a few other cities. Check the timetables before you travel, as services change with the season.

Can I cross into Morocco from here?

Often yes, but check first. Most Western visitors, including British citizens, can stay in Morocco for up to 90 days without a visa. You'll need a valid passport, and you'll get a stamp. But the crossings can close at short notice, queues can be long, and rules change. Many backpackers find it easier to take a ferry to Tangier from Algeciras or Tarifa instead.

What does it cost?

Food and drink are fairly cheap, and a coffee or a small beer costs about €1.50 to €3. A set lunch is usually €10 to €14. Beds cost more than you might expect, because hostels are rare. Compare budget hotels and hostales, and book early.

Do I need to speak Spanish?

It helps a lot. English isn't widely spoken here, and many people speak Spanish or Arabic. Learn a few phrases and use a translation app when you're stuck. People will be glad you tried.

Is the tap water safe?

Yes, it's safe to drink, though it can taste chalky. Many people drink bottled water. Refilling your bottle at your hotel saves money.

Is it safe?

Day to day, yes. Crime against tourists is low. But the borders are a sensitive area, and the situation can change quickly. Please read our full safety section below.

Staying Safe & Travelling Well in Ceuta & Melilla

Most visitors have an easy, safe trip, and these are small, calm cities. But the borders are sensitive, and a few things need extra care. Read this section before you book.

Borders and Current Tensions

In late July 2026, tens of thousands of people crossed from Morocco into Ceuta over about two days. It was the biggest crossing the city has ever seen, and many people died. Reports of the exact numbers vary. Most of those who crossed were returned to Morocco within days. Melilla's main border crossing was also shut for a time. Spain sent in soldiers and extra police, and Morocco tightened security on its side. Both countries still disagree about who owns the cities, so tensions could rise again.

Check your government's latest travel advice and local news before you book, and again before you travel. Expect more police and checks than usual. Carry your passport at all times, be patient at checkpoints, and follow instructions from officers. Don't join protests, and don't film or photograph police, soldiers or border posts.

Stay Away From the Fences and Breakwaters

Keep well clear of the border fences and the breakwaters that stretch into the sea at the border. Some areas are closed, and the water there is dangerous. People have died trying to swim round them. Don't go there to take photos, and don't swim anywhere near them.

Crossing Into Morocco

Use official crossings only, and carry your passport. The border can close with no warning, so plan a back-up and keep enough time and money to get home. Queues can be long and hot. On the Moroccan side, fake guides and touts are common. Agree on prices first, say no politely, and keep your valuables close. Check that your travel insurance covers Morocco.

Swimming and Sea Conditions

The Strait of Gibraltar has strong currents and winds. Swim at beaches with lifeguards when you can, and check the flag: green means fine, yellow means take care, and red means stay out. Jellyfish are common in summer.

Heat, Sun and Wind

Summer is hot, and the sun is strong. Use high-factor sunscreen and drink lots of water. In Ceuta, the levante wind can bring thick fog and damp air, so pack a light jacket even in warm months.

Respect and Culture

These cities are home to people of many faiths, so be respectful. Dress modestly at religious sites, and ask before you photograph people. During Ramadan, avoid eating or drinking in front of people who are fasting, out of courtesy. Don't take sides in arguments about who owns the cities. It's a sore subject for many people.

Petty Theft and Scams

Crime is low, but don't be careless. Watch your bag in busy places and near the ports and borders. Use licensed taxis, and keep your passport safe but within reach.

Looking After the Place

Many residents are worried and tired after a hard summer. Be a kind guest. Take your litter with you and use local shops and cafés. Never photograph people in distress, and treat everyone with respect, whatever their story.

Quick Facts

Best Time To Visit:
April to June and September to October. Summer is hot and sticky, and winter is mild and often wet.
Best For:
History fans, curious travellers, Moorish forts, Modernist streets and day trips into Morocco.
Most Popular Experience:
Walking the old walls of Melilla la Vieja, or Ceuta's Royal Walls and Monte Hacho.
Most Under-rated Experience:
Melilla's Modernist streets, often said to be the best collection in Spain after Barcelona.
Best Free Experiences:
The old walls and ramparts, Monte Hacho's viewpoints, Melilla's Modernist streets and the town beaches.
Ideal Stay:
2 to 3 days in each city, or 4 days if you add a day trip to Morocco.
Budget Level:
Moderate. Hostels are rare, so beds cost more, but food, drink and fuel are cheap. Expect roughly €40–€85 per day.
Getting Around:
Ferry from Algeciras to Ceuta (about an hour). Ferry or flight to Melilla from Málaga, Almería or Madrid. Both cities are small, so you can walk or take a short taxi or bus.
Best Photo Spots:
Ceuta's Royal Walls, the view from Monte Hacho, Melilla la Vieja's ramparts at sunset, and the curved balconies of Melilla's Modernist streets.

1. Fortresses & Old Towns
(Non-Negotiable)

Both cities are built around old forts and walls, and they're the best reason to come. They're free or cheap to explore, and they're far less crowded than on the mainland.

Melilla la Vieja

Melilla's old city is a fortress on a cliff, with four walled sections, cobbled lanes and sea views. It was built between the 1500s and 1700s to keep out attackers. Walk the ramparts, then stop for a drink in a quiet square. Walking is free, and some museums charge a small fee.

Ceuta's Royal Walls & Monte Hacho

Ceuta's Royal Walls run through the middle of the city, with a water-filled moat in front. Above the city, the fortress on Monte Hacho has views across the Strait to Gibraltar. Legend says this hill is one of the Pillars of Hercules. Go late in the day, when the light is soft.

Melilla's Modernist Streets

The architect Enrique Nieto filled Melilla with colourful Modernist buildings in the early 1900s. It's often said to have the best collection in Spain after Barcelona. Wander the streets around Plaza de España and look up at the curved balconies and tiled fronts. It costs nothing, and it's great for photos.

Plaza de África, Ceuta

The heart of Ceuta's old centre, with a cathedral, churches and grand old buildings. Sit at a café terrace and watch the city go by. It's a good place to start, and to end, a day of sightseeing.

2. Adrenaline

Diving: The waters around Ceuta are rich in marine life, where the Atlantic meets the Mediterranean. Local dive schools run trips for beginners and experienced divers.

Kayaking and paddleboarding: Calm mornings are perfect for a paddle along the coast. Hire a kayak or board from a beach club, and stay well away from the border breakwaters.

Hiking Monte Hacho: A coastal trail with big views of the Strait leads round the hill. It's easy and free, and you might spot ships passing between two seas.

Cycling the seafront: Both cities have flat seafront routes, and bikes are easy to hire. It's a lovely way to see a lot in a short time.

3. Beaches & Sea Days (The Days That Change Everything)

The beaches here are small and local, not wild, but they're free, easy to reach and often quiet.

Ceuta's Beaches

Playa de la Ribera and Playa del Chorrillo are town beaches with sand, cafés and promenades. Calamocarro is quieter and further out. Check the flags and swim where the lifeguards are.

Melilla's Beaches

Los Cárabos and La Hípica are the main town beaches, with sand, showers and beach bars in summer. They're close to the centre, so you can swim and be back in the old town in minutes.

Parque Marítimo del Mediterráneo, Ceuta

A seaside leisure park with saltwater pools and gardens, designed by the Canarian artist César Manrique. It's a fun place to spend a hot afternoon. Entry costs a few euros.

A note on swimming: Never swim near the border fences or breakwaters. The water there is dangerous, and some areas are closed.

4. Free Ceuta & Melilla (Zero Euros)

The old walls and ramparts: Walking round Melilla la Vieja and Ceuta's Royal Walls costs nothing. Sunset on the ramparts is the best free show in town.

Monte Hacho's viewpoints: Walk or drive up for sweeping views of the Strait and Gibraltar on a clear day.

A Modernist walking tour: Wander the streets of Melilla and spot the curved balconies, tiles and towers. All you need is a camera.

The beaches: The town beaches are free. Bring a towel, water and some shade.

Plazas and parks: Plaza de África in Ceuta and Parque Hernández in Melilla are free to enjoy. Both are ideal places to sit with a cheap coffee and watch the world go by.

Fiestas and processions: Local festivals spill onto the streets, with music, food and parades. Watching is free.

5. Nightlife: How It Actually Works

Nights here are quieter than in most of Spain. Expect terraces, tapas bars and small clubs, with the busiest evenings on summer weekends. Many Muslim neighbourhoods are quiet at night, and some cafés don't serve alcohol. Drinks are often cheap, as taxes are lower.

For a first drink

Pick a terrace in a plaza in either city. Order a small beer, or "caña", and some tapas, and sit back. It's relaxed, friendly and good value.

For something different

Try a Moroccan-style tea house, where you can sip sweet mint tea and nibble pastries. Many serve grilled meat skewers, known as "pinchitos morunos", which are great for a cheap dinner.

For a late one

On summer weekends, a few bars and clubs stay open until the early hours. Ask your hotel or locals which are busy that night. Take a licensed taxi home.

6. The Big Hits (Things You Must Not Skip)

Melilla la Vieja

Covered in full above, and it's the single best sight in either city. Go at sunset, when the stone glows gold.

Ceuta's Royal Walls

Easy to reach and fun to explore, with its moat and ramparts right in the centre of the city.

Monte Hacho

The best view in Ceuta, and a legendary Pillar of Hercules. Go on a clear day to see the coast of Europe across the water.

Day trip: Tetouan or Chefchaouen from Ceuta

If the border is open and you feel comfortable, a day trip into Morocco is a real adventure. Tetouan has a lovely old medina, and Chefchaouen is famous for its blue streets. Check the current border rules and your travel insurance first.

7. Live Sport & Culture

Local football: AD Ceuta and UD Melilla play in the lower leagues. Tickets are cheap, and the crowds are friendly.

Carnival in Ceuta: Each year, the city holds a lively carnival with costumes, music and parades. It's a fun time to visit, and it's mostly free to watch.

Summer and September fiestas: Both cities hold big local fiestas in late summer. There are parades, music, fairs and fireworks. Book your bed early.

Eid and Ramadan: The cities' Muslim communities celebrate with food, family and prayer. During Ramadan, evenings after sunset are lively, with plenty of food on offer.
