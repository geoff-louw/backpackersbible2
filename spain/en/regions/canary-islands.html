Backpacking the Canary Islands

[EES-lahs kah-NAH-ree-ahs]

On a map, the Canary Islands look lost. They sit in the Atlantic, about 100 km off the coast of Morocco and more than 1,000 km from mainland Spain. Yet they're every bit as Spanish as paella. They're also nothing like the Spain you've seen. Think black volcanic beaches, a desert of fire mountains, misty forests and a giant volcano that pokes above the clouds. And they're warm all year, with spring-like weather even in January.

For backpackers from the UK, the Canaries are a dream. The flight takes about four hours, and the islands run on UK time, so there's no jet lag. Each island has its own personality. Tenerife has the volcano and the party resorts. Gran Canaria has a big, fun city and a sea of sand dunes. Lanzarote and Fuerteventura are famous for surf, wind and wide empty beaches. La Palma, La Gomera and El Hierro are the quiet, green islands for hikers and stargazers.

It's good budget travel too. The Canaries have lower taxes than mainland Spain, so food, drinks and car hire often cost less. Hostels are fewer than in Barcelona or Madrid, but you'll find them in the surf towns and the main cities. Book early in winter, when northern Europe flocks here for the sun. Many backpackers come for one island and end up hopping to three, because the cheap flights and ferries make it so easy.

What Kind of Place Is This, Exactly?

The Canary Islands are a group of volcanic islands in the Atlantic Ocean, west of Morocco. About 2.2 million people live here. There are eight main islands. The biggest are Tenerife and Gran Canaria, followed by Fuerteventura and Lanzarote. Then come La Palma, La Gomera, El Hierro and tiny La Graciosa. They're an official part of Spain and the EU, but with their own tax rules. The two biggest cities, Las Palmas on Gran Canaria and Santa Cruz on Tenerife, share the role of regional capital.

A Short History: Dogs, Guanches and Columbus

The first people to live here were the Guanches, who probably came from North Africa thousands of years ago. The Romans heard about the islands, and the name may come from the Latin word for dogs, because of the big wild dogs they found. The canary bird was named after the islands, not the other way around. Spain took control in the 1400s, and the last island, Tenerife, fell in 1496. Columbus stopped on La Gomera in 1492 before he sailed west. Ever since, the Canaries have had strong ties with Latin America, and many islanders emigrated to Cuba and Venezuela.

The Language You'll Hear

Spanish, but it sounds more like Latin America than Madrid. Canarians say "ustedes" instead of "vosotros", and they call a bus a "guagua". They also say "papas" for potatoes. English is widely spoken in the resorts and in hostels. Away from the tourist areas, a few Spanish words go a long way, and locals are friendly and proud of their islands.

Canary Islands or Canarias?

They're the same place. Canarias is the Spanish name, and you'll see it on road signs, ferry tickets and maps. Canary Islands is the English name. The Spanish word for a Canarian person is "canario", and the islanders are very proud of their identity. Many locals see themselves as Canarian first and Spanish second.

Understanding the Canaries: The Islands & Zones

Tenerife

The biggest island has the most variety. Mount Teide, the highest mountain in Spain, towers over the middle. The south is hot and dry, and it has the party resorts of Playa de las Américas and Los Cristianos. The north is greener, with cloud forests, hiking trails and the lively students' town of La Laguna.

Gran Canaria

Often called a continent in miniature. Las Palmas is a big, fun city with a great city beach, and it's the best base for backpackers. In the south, the Maspalomas dunes look like a small Sahara, and nearby Playa del Inglés is a huge resort. In the middle, the mountains hide rocky peaks and tiny villages.

Lanzarote

The black island. Volcanoes erupted here in the 1700s and covered a quarter of the land in lava. The result is a wild, moon-like landscape with white villages and vineyards growing in pits. The artist César Manrique shaped the island's look, and strict rules keep out high-rises. Famara is the surf town, while Puerto del Carmen is the party strip.

Fuerteventura

The windy island, with the longest and emptiest beaches in the Canaries. Corralejo and El Cotillo are laid-back surf towns with a young crowd. Sotavento beach is a world-famous spot for windsurfing and kitesurfing. The interior is dry, quiet and almost empty.

La Palma

Green, steep and wild. It has one of the best night skies in the world, plus a huge crater called the Caldera de Taburiente. In 2021 the Cumbre Vieja volcano erupted for almost three months, and you can now join guided walks over the new lava fields.

La Gomera, El Hierro & La Graciosa

The quiet islands. La Gomera has a misty UNESCO forest and Valle Gran Rey, an old hippy valley. El Hierro is tiny and remote, with brilliant diving and sleepy villages. La Graciosa, off Lanzarote, has sandy streets instead of roads. Come here to switch off.

A Note on the Weather

The weather changes a lot from place to place. The south of each island is usually dry and sunny. The north is greener and often cloudy, because winds from the Atlantic bring moisture. Locals call the layer of cloud the "sea of clouds", and you can climb above it and into bright sun. Always carry a layer, even on a hot day.

Canary Islands FAQs for Backpackers

When is the best time to go?

Any time. The weather is mild all year, with highs of about 20 to 28°C. Winter is the busiest and priciest time, because Europeans come for the sun. Summer is hotter, quieter and cheaper in some places, but it's also dry, and wildfires can happen. Spring and autumn are a good mix of warm weather and fewer crowds.

Which island should I pick?

For parties and a big range of things to do, pick Tenerife or Gran Canaria. For surf and wind sports, pick Fuerteventura or Lanzarote. For hiking, stars and peace, pick La Palma, La Gomera or El Hierro. If you have two weeks, try two islands. It's easy to hop by cheap flight or ferry.

Is it really Spain?

Yes, but it's very different. The islands are part of Spain and the EU, but they run on UK time, one hour behind the mainland. They have lower taxes, so prices are often lower too. They're closer to Africa than to Europe, which is why the weather is so warm. The accent, the food and the landscape feel different from the mainland.

What does it cost?

It's cheaper than the Balearics and many mainland cities. A small beer costs about €1.50 to €3, a local lunch about €10 to €14, and a cheap car hire can cost less than a bus pass. Resorts cost more than local towns. Cook in your hostel to save even more.

How do I get around?

Buses, called guaguas, are good on Tenerife and Gran Canaria. Hire cars are cheap, and sharing one with people from your hostel is a great way to see the wild parts. To move between islands, take a cheap flight or a ferry. On La Gomera and El Hierro, buses are rare, so plan ahead.

Do I need to speak Spanish?

Not in the resorts, where English is common. In smaller villages, Spanish helps a lot. Learn the basics and try them out. Locals will thank you for it.

Is the tap water safe?

It's safe to drink, but it often comes from the sea and can taste salty or chalky. Most people drink bottled water. Fill a big bottle at your hostel to save money, and don't waste water, because the islands have little of it.

Is it safe?

Yes. Violent crime against tourists is rare. The real risks are the Atlantic waves, the strong sun, the mountain weather and summer wildfires. See our full safety section below.

Staying Safe & Travelling Well in the Canary Islands

The Canary Islands are safe for backpackers. Crime is low, and the police are used to young visitors. Most of the dangers come from nature, so respect the sea, the sun and the mountains and you'll be fine.

Atlantic Currents and Waves

The Atlantic is much stronger than the Mediterranean. Many beaches have big waves and strong currents, especially on the north coasts. Always check the flag: green means fine, yellow means take care, and red means stay out. Rocky natural pools can be swept by sudden waves, so never swim in them in rough seas. La Graciosa's Playa de las Conchas is beautiful but has dangerous currents, so look and don't swim. If you see a blue bubble on the sand or in the water, it may be a Portuguese man o' war, so keep away.

Sun and Altitude

The sun is strong all year, even when a cool breeze hides it. Wear high-factor sunscreen and a hat. Mount Teide is 3,715 metres high, so the air is thin and cold, and it can snow in winter. Take warm clothes, drink water, and turn back if you feel dizzy or sick. The final stretch to the summit needs a free permit, so apply online well before you go.

Hiking Safety

Canary trails are spectacular but steep. Weather can change fast, and cloud or mist can hide the path. Carry plenty of water, snacks, a charged phone and a layer. Stay on marked paths, and start early so you finish before dark. Avoid ravines, called barrancos, when it rains, as they can flood quickly.

Wildfires and Dust

Summer can be hot and dry, and wildfires have burned parts of Tenerife and Gran Canaria. Never light a fire or throw a cigarette in the countryside. Some forests and parks close when the risk is high, so check before you go. Now and then, a hot, dusty wind from the Sahara, called the calima, covers the islands in haze. Stay indoors and keep your water bottle full.

Nightlife and Petty Theft

The party resorts are lively and mostly safe, but keep your wits about you. Watch your drink, as spiking can happen in any crowded bar. Street sellers in resort areas offer drugs, and it's best to say no. In Spain, using or carrying drugs in public brings fines, and fake pills can be dangerous. Never leave valuables in a hire car at a beach or trailhead, as break-ins happen in remote places.

Driving on Island Roads

Mountain roads are narrow, winding and steep, with sharp drops and often thick cloud. Take your time and don't drive after drinking, as Spain's limit is lower than in England and Wales. Think carefully before hiring a scooter, and always wear a helmet.

Looking After the Islands

Many locals say mass tourism has pushed up housing costs and damaged nature, and thousands have protested. Be a good guest. Stay on marked paths, as the volcanic soil and dunes are fragile. Don't take volcanic stones or sand home, since it's illegal and can bring a fine. If you go whale watching, pick a boat with the "Barco Azul" label, which means it follows the rules to protect the animals. Don't camp in the wild, as that's banned, and use bins for your rubbish.

Quick Facts

Best Time To Visit:
Any time of year. Winter is busiest and priciest, summer is hotter and drier, and spring and autumn are a good mix.
Best For:
Volcanic landscapes, surf and wind sports, hiking, stargazing and winter sun.
Most Popular Experience:
Climbing Mount Teide, the highest mountain in Spain, and watching the stars from the summit area.
Most Under-rated Experience:
La Palma's Caldera de Taburiente and its sky full of stars, far from the party resorts.
Best Free Experiences:
The Maspalomas dunes, Las Canteras beach, hiking Mount Teide and the Anaga forest, and the Carnival street parties.
Ideal Stay:
10 to 14 days: spend 4 or 5 days on each of two or three islands.
Budget Level:
Cheaper than most of mainland Spain and much cheaper than the Balearics. Expect roughly €35–€80 per day, and more in the big resorts.
Getting Around:
Buses (guaguas) are good on Tenerife and Gran Canaria, and hire cars are cheap. Hop between islands by cheap flight or ferry.
Best Photo Spots:
Mount Teide at sunrise, Roque Nublo on Gran Canaria, the volcanic landscape of Timanfaya, and the Maspalomas and Corralejo dunes.

1. Volcano Country: Teide, Timanfaya & Beyond
(Non-Negotiable)

The Canaries were built by volcanoes, and the landscapes are like nowhere else in Europe. Two sights should be on every list.

Mount Teide, Tenerife

At 3,715 metres, Teide is the highest mountain in Spain and sits in a UNESCO World Heritage national park. Take the cable car, or hike up through a weird landscape of lava and rock. The final stretch to the summit needs a free permit, so book online well ahead. Come at sunrise or sunset for the best views.

Timanfaya National Park, Lanzarote

The Fire Mountains look like another planet, with red and black craters stretching to the sea. Join a bus tour through the park, or watch guides pour water into the ground and see it explode into steam. There's an entry fee, but it's worth it.

Read More

Roque Nublo, Gran Canaria

A huge needle of rock, 80 metres high, towering over the island's centre. It's an easy walk of about an hour from the car park, and the views stretch across the whole island. Go in the morning, before the clouds roll in.

César Manrique's Lanzarote

The artist César Manrique turned the island's caves and cliffs into art. Jameos del Agua is a lava tunnel with a pool and a concert hall. The Mirador del Río is a clifftop viewpoint with an amazing view of La Graciosa. Entry costs a few euros.

2. Adrenaline

Surfing, windsurfing and kitesurfing: Fuerteventura and Lanzarote have some of Europe's best wind and waves. Corralejo, El Cotillo and Famara have schools for beginners. Sotavento beach is world famous for windsurfing and kitesurfing.

Hiking the volcanoes and ravines: Tenerife, Gran Canaria and La Palma have huge hiking networks, from easy forest walks to tough climbs. You can walk for days without paying a cent.

Diving: The water is clear and warm, with rays, turtles and volcanic reefs. El Hierro's La Restinga is a top spot, and Tenerife and Fuerteventura have plenty of dive schools.

Whale and dolphin watching: Pilot whales live off the south coast of Tenerife all year. Pick a boat with the Barco Azul label, so the animals are treated well.

3. The Quiet Islands (The Days That Change Everything)

Most people stay on the big, busy islands. The small ones are where backpackers often fall in love with the Canaries.

La Palma

Climb to the rim of the Caldera de Taburiente, a giant crater with pine forests and waterfalls. At night the sky fills with stars, as La Palma is one of the best places in the world to see them. You can also join a guided walk across the lava fields from the 2021 eruption.

La Gomera

Take the ferry from Tenerife and explore Garajonay, a misty UNESCO forest. Valle Gran Rey is a green valley with black beaches, a laid-back hippy crowd and cheap places to eat. Islanders once spoke across the valleys with a whistled language called Silbo Gomero, and some still do.

El Hierro

The smallest and most remote island, once thought to be the end of the world. It has sleepy villages, rock pools and some of the best diving in Spain. Bring a book and a slow pace.

A note on La Graciosa: This tiny island off Lanzarote has no paved roads, only sand tracks. Take the 25-minute ferry from Órzola, hire a bike and ride to the beaches. Remember that the currents at Playa de las Conchas make swimming dangerous.

4. Free Canary Islands (Zero Euros)

The Maspalomas dunes, Gran Canaria: Walk through a huge area of sand dunes that looks like a piece of the Sahara. It's a protected nature reserve, and it's free to enter.

Las Canteras beach, Las Palmas: One of the best city beaches in Spain, with calm water thanks to a natural reef. A long promenade is lined with cheap cafés and bars.

Hiking Teide and the Anaga forest: Walking trails in the national park and the mossy Anaga mountains cost nothing. You only need a permit for the final climb to Teide's summit, and it's free.

Stargazing: The skies are among the clearest in Europe. Find a dark spot on Tenerife, La Palma or Fuerteventura, lie back and watch. All you need is a warm jacket.

Carnival street parties: In February, Santa Cruz de Tenerife has one of the biggest carnivals in the world, with parades, music and costumes. Most of the street parties are free.

Beaches and dunes: Corralejo's dunes in Fuerteventura and the long beaches of Lanzarote cost nothing. Bring water and shade, as many have no facilities.

5. Nightlife: How It Actually Works

Nights here follow Spanish hours, a bit more relaxed than the mainland. Dinner is at 9pm or later, bars fill up around 11pm, and clubs get busy after midnight. Many resort bars open early and close late.

For a first drink

In Las Palmas, try the old streets of Triana and Vegueta. In Tenerife, the bars in La Laguna are packed with students. Try a "barraquito", a layered coffee with condensed milk and liqueur, or a cold Dorada beer.

For the party resorts

Playa de las Américas in Tenerife has the Veronicas strip, with loud bars and clubs. Playa del Inglés on Gran Canaria and Puerto del Carmen on Lanzarote are similar. Drinks are cheaper than in Ibiza, and the crowd is a mix of young Brits, Germans and Spaniards.

For LGBTQ+ travellers

Maspalomas on Gran Canaria is one of the biggest gay holiday spots in Europe. The Yumbo Centre in Playa del Inglés is the heart of the scene, and the Pride week in May is a huge party.

For a calmer night

Corralejo, El Cotillo, Famara and Valle Gran Rey have a relaxed, friendly beach-bar scene. Share a bottle of local wine and watch the stars.

6. The Big Hits (Things You Must Not Skip)

Mount Teide

Covered in full above, and it's the single best thing to do in the Canaries. Book your summit permit early.

Timanfaya National Park

The most otherworldly landscape in Spain. Combine it with a visit to La Geria, where vines grow in volcanic pits.

The Maspalomas Dunes

Free, huge and very photogenic, especially at sunrise or sunset. Pack water, as there's no shade.

Day trip: La Gomera from Tenerife

Take the ferry from Los Cristianos and be in La Gomera within an hour. Hike in the misty Garajonay forest, then eat papas arrugadas, the local salty wrinkled potatoes with a spicy sauce, at a village café.

7. Live Sport & Culture

The Canarian derby: CD Tenerife against UD Las Palmas is one of the fiercest local football rivalries in Spain. Tickets are usually cheaper than on the mainland, and the atmosphere is great.

Canarian wrestling: Lucha canaria is a traditional sport where two teams of wrestlers try to throw each other to the ground. It's cheap or free to watch in village arenas, and it's a great way to meet locals.

Carnival and Los Indianos: Santa Cruz's carnival in February is one of the biggest in the world. On La Palma, the Los Indianos party covers the town in clouds of white talcum powder.

Windsurf and kitesurf contests: Pro contests are often held at Sotavento, Fuerteventura, in summer. Watching from the beach is free.

La Rama, Gran Canaria: Every August, the village of Agaete holds the Fiesta de la Rama. People wave leafy branches and dance through the streets to ask for rain.
