Backpacking Valencia

[bah-LEN-syah]

Ask ten backpackers touring Spain what they know about Valencia, and most will say paella — correctly, it was invented here — and then draw a blank. That blank is the whole opportunity. Spain's third-largest city has beaches as good as Barcelona's, nightlife that runs just as late, and a futuristic arts complex that looks like it was airlifted in from another planet, and somehow still isn't on the standard backpacking Spain route. Get there before that changes.

For backpackers coming from the UK or elsewhere in Western Europe, Valencia is genuinely easy to reach and even easier to get around — direct flights land close to the centre, and it's a flat, compact, bike-friendly city with none of Madrid's hills or Barcelona's sprawl. It's also noticeably calmer and safer-feeling than either of Spain's two biggest cities, without losing any of the energy — the beach sits right in the city rather than a day trip away, and once a year, mid-March, the whole place erupts into Las Fallas, one of the loudest, strangest, most spectacular festivals anywhere in the country.

On cost, backpacking Valencia stacks up brilliantly against Barcelona and Madrid — dorms, tapas, and drinks all run noticeably cheaper here, without any drop in quality. It's a genuinely underrated stop for backpacking Spain on a budget, and one of the easiest cities in the whole country to just show up in and figure it out as you go.

What Kind of Place Is This, Exactly?

Valencia is both a city and the autonomous community (Comunidad Valenciana) it anchors, stretching along Spain's central Mediterranean coast between Catalonia and Murcia. The city itself has around 800,000 people, making it Spain's third-largest after Madrid and Barcelona, and it's built around one genuinely strange piece of urban planning: after a catastrophic flood in 1957, the Túria River was diverted entirely around the city, and its old riverbed through the centre was turned into a 9km sunken park — one of the largest and best urban green spaces in Europe, and the site of the city's most famous modern landmark.

A Short History: From El Cid to Calatrava

Valencia has Roman roots as Valentia, then passed through Moorish rule as Balansiya, briefly captured by the legendary Castilian knight El Cid in 1094 before Moorish forces retook it after his death. The definitive Christian conquest came in 1238 under James I of Aragon, and the Kingdom of Valencia that followed became part of the Crown of Aragon, with its own laws and language much like Catalonia. A 15th- and 16th-century trading boom — visible today in the Silk Exchange (Llotja de la Seda), a UNESCO World Heritage building — made Valencia one of the wealthiest cities in the Mediterranean. Its most recent reinvention came after that 1957 flood: the drained riverbed eventually became home to Santiago Calatrava's City of Arts and Sciences, transforming Valencia's skyline and international profile almost overnight.

The Language You'll Hear

Both Castilian Spanish and Valencian (Valencià) are official here. Valencian is closely related to Catalan — linguists generally treat it as a variant of the same language, though plenty of Valencians will tell you firmly that it's its own distinct tongue, a genuinely live point of local identity. Day to day, Spanish dominates in the city itself, with Valencian more prominent in smaller towns and rural areas. English is spoken reasonably well in tourist areas and hostels.

Understanding Valencia: The Zones

Ciutat Vella (The Old Town)

The historic core — the Silk Exchange, the cathedral (which claims to hold the actual Holy Grail), and the Central Market, one of the oldest and largest covered food markets in Europe. Compact, walkable, and where most hostels cluster.

The City of Arts and Sciences & The Túria Gardens

Calatrava's futuristic complex — an aquarium, a planetarium, a science museum, an opera house — sitting at the end of the old riverbed-turned-park that runs almost the entire length of the city. The gardens alone are worth a full afternoon, on foot or by bike.

The Beaches: Malvarrosa & Las Arenas

A proper, wide, sandy city beach a short tram ride from the centre, lined with paella restaurants and beach bars — genuinely one of Valencia's biggest advantages over Madrid, and arguably as good as anything Barcelona offers.

The Costa Blanca

South of the city, the Costa Blanca runs through Alicante and the infamous package-holiday resort of Benidorm — not really backpacker territory, but worth knowing about, if only for the novelty of seeing Spain's most self-aware built-for-tourists skyline in person.

L'Albufera Natural Park

A freshwater lagoon and wetland just south of the city, surrounded by the rice paddies that make Valencia's paella what it is — a boat trip across the water at sunset is one of the most peaceful things you can do near any major Spanish city.

Valencia FAQs for Backpackers

When is the best time to go?

April, May, June, September, and October are all excellent — warm without Andalusia-level extremes. Mid-March brings Las Fallas, one of Spain's biggest festivals (see below), which is worth timing a trip around if huge crowds and constant noise sound appealing rather than exhausting.

Where should I stay?

Ciutat Vella (the old town) is the classic backpacker base — central, walkable, hostel-heavy. Ruzafa, just south of the centre, is trendier and increasingly the go-to for nightlife and good, affordable food.

What does it cost to go out?

Cheap by Spanish standards — a beer runs roughly €2–€3.50, and Ruzafa in particular has a strong, affordable bar and tapas scene.

Do I need to speak Spanish?

English gets you through the main sights and hostels fine, but a little Spanish helps once you're off the tourist track — Valencian is mostly a bonus rather than a necessity for visitors.

How do I get around?

Valencia is flat, compact, and one of the most bike-friendly cities in Spain — Valenbisi, the public bike-share scheme, is an easy, cheap way to cover ground fast, especially through the Túria Gardens. A metro and tram network (including a direct line to the beach) covers everything else.

Is the tap water safe?

Yes, throughout Valencia. Perfectly safe to drink.

Is Valencia safe?

Yes — Valencia is one of the lower-key, lower-crime major cities in Spain, with a noticeably calmer feel than Barcelona or even Madrid. See our full safety section below for the one genuine exception: Las Fallas.

What actually is Las Fallas?

A mid-March festival built around giant, elaborate sculptures (fallas) constructed by neighbourhood committees all year round, then set on fire in a single spectacular night, la cremà. Alongside the sculptures, daily mascletà displays — synchronised, deafeningly loud daytime firework-and-firecracker shows — go off in the main square at 2pm sharp. It's genuinely one of the most intense sensory experiences in European festival culture, and not for anyone who dislikes loud, sudden noise.

If it sounds a little like Burning Man, that's no coincidence in spirit, if not in origin. Both festivals combine spectacular art, communal creativity and the ritual destruction of enormous sculptures by fire. Burning Man developed independently in California, but the parallels are so striking that the two festivals have since exchanged visits and ideas. The difference? At Las Fallas, the spectacle unfolds across an entire historic European city, with neighbourhoods competing to create ever more extraordinary works of art — only to watch them go up in flames.

Is the paella here actually different?

Yes, and locals take it seriously. Authentic Valencian paella is made with chicken and rabbit, not seafood — the seafood version popular elsewhere in Spain (and abroad) is a later variation. Order "paella valenciana" for the real, original thing.

Staying Safe & Travelling Well in Valencia

Valencia is one of the safer, lower-key major cities in Spain for backpackers — noticeably calmer than Barcelona, with none of the pickpocketing reputation and no significant overtourism backlash. The one real exception to that calm is Las Fallas, which brings its own specific set of things to know.

Las Fallas Fire and Crowd Safety

If you're in Valencia for Las Fallas, take it seriously: the mascletà firecracker displays are genuinely deafening at close range (earplugs are a good idea), the final night's bonfires burn enormous wooden-and-cardboard sculptures in tight city streets, and crowds get dense fast. Keep a firm eye on your group, stay aware of your surroundings near open flames, and don't assume a quiet side street will stay quiet — fireworks go off unpredictably all over the city for the whole week.

Petty Theft

A much smaller issue here than in Barcelona or Madrid, but standard precautions still apply in crowded tourist spots like the Central Market and around the City of Arts and Sciences.

Nightlife Scams and Drink Safety

Watch for unofficial club promoters offering deals that turn into inflated bar tabs, and never leave a drink unattended — standard advice anywhere, but Ruzafa and the beach clubs get busy enough in summer that it's worth repeating.

Beach Safety

Malvarrosa and Las Arenas are generally calm, well-supervised beaches with lifeguards in season — check flag warnings as with any beach, but this isn't the rough Atlantic coast further south.

Quick Facts

Best Time To Visit:
April to June and September to October — plus mid-March if you want to catch Las Fallas.
Best For:
City beaches, futuristic architecture, real paella, cheap tapas, and a genuinely sociable hostel scene.
Most Popular Experience:
Wandering the City of Arts and Sciences after dark, when the buildings are lit up and reflected in the pools.
Most Under-rated Experience:
A sunset boat trip across L'Albufera — most visitors never leave the city centre long enough to see it.
Best Free Experiences:
The Túria Gardens, Malvarrosa beach, the Central Market's browsing (not buying), and the free exterior architecture of the City of Arts and Sciences.
Ideal Stay:
3–4 days for the city itself, longer if your dates land on Las Fallas.
Budget Level:
Noticeably cheaper than Barcelona or Madrid. Expect roughly €35–€75 per day.
Getting Around:
Flat, compact, and one of Spain's most bike-friendly cities — Valenbisi bike-share plus a metro and tram network covering everything, including the beach.
Best Photo Spots:
The City of Arts and Sciences reflected in its pools, the Silk Exchange's twisted columns, Malvarrosa beach at sunrise, and the Central Market's stained-glass roof.

1. The City of Arts and Sciences & The Old Town (Non-Negotiable)

Valencia's two defining sights couldn't be more different from each other, and that contrast is a big part of what makes the city work.

The City of Arts and Sciences

Santiago Calatrava's futuristic complex at the end of the old riverbed — the Hemisfèric planetarium, the Príncipe Felipe Science Museum, and L'Oceanogràfic, Europe's largest aquarium, complete with the continent's longest underwater tunnel. You can appreciate the architecture from outside for free, but the Oceanogràfic alone is worth the entry price. Combo tickets from roughly €45.

The Silk Exchange & Central Market

The Llotja de la Seda, a UNESCO-listed Gothic trading hall from Valencia's silk-boom golden age, sits directly across from the Central Market — one of Europe's oldest and largest covered food markets, still fully working, under a genuinely spectacular stained-glass dome.

Valencia Cathedral

Built on the site of a former mosque, and home to a chalice that the Vatican itself recognises as a plausible candidate for the actual Holy Grail — worth the climb up the Miguelete bell tower for the rooftop view alone.

2. Adrenaline

Cycling the Túria Gardens: Nine kilometres of car-free park running almost the full length of the city — genuinely one of the best urban cycling routes in Europe, easily covered on a rented Valenbisi bike.

Kayaking or paddleboarding L'Albufera: Paddle the freshwater lagoon south of the city through the rice paddies that produce Valencia's famous paella rice.

Watersports at Malvarrosa Beach: Jet-skiing, paddleboarding, and sailing lessons are all easily arranged right off the city beach.

3. The Beaches & The Túria Gardens

Unlike Madrid, and arguably rivalling Barcelona, Valencia is a genuine beach city — and the old riverbed running through its centre gives it a second, entirely different kind of green escape.

Malvarrosa & Las Arenas

A wide, proper sandy beach a short tram ride from the old town, lined with paella restaurants (this is the place to order the real, original chicken-and-rabbit version) and low-key beach bars.

The Túria Gardens

A sunken park running the length of the old riverbed — running tracks, football pitches, playgrounds, and shaded walking paths, all free and all connecting the old town directly to the City of Arts and Sciences.

4. Free Valencia (Zero Euros)

The Túria Gardens: The whole 9km park costs nothing and is genuinely one of the best things in the city.

City of Arts and Sciences exteriors: You don't need to pay to admire Calatrava's buildings from outside — genuinely striking, especially reflected in the surrounding pools at dusk.

The Central Market: Wandering (not buying) costs nothing, and the building itself is worth the visit alone.

Malvarrosa Beach: Free, public, and genuinely good — no need to pay for a beach club to enjoy a day by the water.

5. Nightlife: How It Actually Works

Valencia keeps Spanish hours without Madrid's full-throttle intensity — dinner from 9–10pm, bars filling from 11pm, and a genuinely good, less chaotic night out than Barcelona or Madrid at their busiest.

For a first drink

Ruzafa is the obvious starting point — a trendy, increasingly hip neighbourhood south of the centre with a strong bar and small-plates scene.

For clubbing

The old town and the port area both have solid late-night options, and in summer the beach clubs along Malvarrosa run outdoor sessions well past midnight.

For something more local

The old town's smaller squares — particularly around Plaza del Tossal — have a proper local bar-hopping scene once you get off the main tourist streets.

6. The Big Hits (Things You Must Not Skip)

Las Fallas (if your dates line up)

Giant sculptures, deafening daily firecracker displays, and a final night of citywide bonfires — one of the most spectacular festivals in Spain. See the FAQ and Safety sections above for the full detail.

L'Oceanogràfic

Europe's largest aquarium, and the standout attraction within the City of Arts and Sciences — covered in full above.

The Silk Exchange

Also covered above, but worth repeating: a genuine architectural highlight and one of the best-preserved Gothic civil buildings anywhere in Spain.

L'Albufera at Sunset

A short bus ride from the city, a boat trip across the lagoon at golden hour is one of the most underrated experiences near any major Spanish city — see the zones section above for more.

7. Live Sport

Valencia CF at the Mestalla: One of Spain's most historic football grounds and a genuinely good, comparatively affordable way to catch live La Liga football — tickets for standard fixtures are noticeably cheaper than Barcelona or Madrid.

Levante UD: The city's second club, with a smaller but fiercely loyal fanbase — a match against Valencia CF (the "derbi valencià") is one of Spain's lesser-known but genuinely intense local derbies.
