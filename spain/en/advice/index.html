Advice For Backpackers

Essential Travel Advice for Backpackers

Spain is one of the easiest countries in Europe to backpack — cheap set-price lunches, a superb rail network, a hostel in every town worth visiting and a culture that rewards staying out late. It is also a country where the small print matters. The rules for getting in have just been rebuilt, the summer heat is a genuine hazard, professional pickpockets work the famous streets, and a handful of local laws catch out visitors who assume that "Europe" means one set of rules. Sort the boring things out before you fly and Spain is forgiving; ignore them and you can lose a day — or a passport, a phone or a fine — very quickly.

This page covers everything to organise before you leave: entry rules, safety, money, health, embassies, drinking and drugs law, and the apps worth installing. Once you are on the ground, head to the in-country advice for transport, food, language, etiquette and emergencies; for surfing, the Camino, cycling, language schools and more, see the specific-interest advice.

A note on how current this is: the entry rules, outbreak figures and laws below were checked against official government and health-agency sources in October 2026, and prices are planning ranges rather than quoted tariffs. Rules for non-EU visitors are changing quickly, so where something is flagged as "check before you travel", please do.

Preflight Checklists

Passport rules, insurance and bookings are tedious to sort out at home and miserable to sort out at a check-in desk or a border booth. Work through these in the six to eight weeks before you leave.

1. PASSPORTS, VISAS AND ENTRY

Visa-free entry: Citizens of the UK, USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, Brazil, Argentina, Mexico and many other countries can enter Spain without a visa for up to 90 days in any 180-day period. Spain follows Schengen rules, so that allowance is shared across the whole Schengen area, not per country: days spent in France, Portugal, Italy and the rest all count against the same 90. Count back 180 days from every planned entry, or use the European Commission's short-stay calculator. Overstaying can lead to a ban of up to three years.

If you need a visa: Some nationalities need a Schengen visa even for a short holiday. South African passport holders do — the Spanish consulate in Cape Town states this plainly — and in South Africa applications are lodged in person through BLS International's Spain visa centres. The medical insurance attached to a Schengen visa application must cover at least €30,000, including repatriation and emergency hospital treatment, for the whole stay. Apply early, book your appointment before you buy non-refundable flights, and check the current document list on the Spanish embassy or consulate website for your country. South African readers can find the Spanish embassy's details on our South Africa advice page.

Passport validity: For entry to the Schengen area your passport must have been issued less than 10 years before the day you arrive and must expire at least three months after the day you plan to leave. The first rule catches people out: a UK passport renewed before October 2018 could carry extra months over from the old one, so it can look valid for years and still fail the 10-year test. Check both dates now, and check them again against your latest possible departure date.

The EU Entry/Exit System (EES): Passport stamps are being replaced by a digital border record. On your first entry you will be asked to register a facial photo and fingerprints (children aged 11 and under give a photo only). It costs nothing, the record lasts three years, and you may be asked for a photo or fingerprint again on later entries and when you leave. The system has been fully operational at Schengen borders since April 2026. The practical consequence is time: allow extra at passport control on arrival, and — because checks can happen before boarding — extra on departure too. Do not book tight connections through Spanish airports.

ETIAS — check before you book: ETIAS is a separate, online travel authorisation that visa-exempt visitors will have to obtain before travelling to Schengen countries. As of early October 2026 it is not yet in operation; the EU's official line is that it will start in the last quarter of 2026, with the exact date announced in advance and a transition period before it becomes compulsory. The fee is €20 for applicants aged 18 to 70 and the authorisation lasts three years. Do not apply through third-party websites — copycat sites charging inflated fees exist. The only place to apply, and to check whether it has started, is the EU's official travel site: travel-europe.europa.eu/etias.

What border officers can ask for: A return or onward ticket, proof of travel insurance, proof that you can fund your stay, and your accommodation booking (or an invitation from a host). The money test is a real rule even though it is rarely enforced: Spanish regulations give an indicative figure of roughly €113 per person per day. If you are asked, a bank-app screenshot or a card with a healthy balance is normally enough. Keep your first night's booking and your onward ticket on your phone and on paper.

Under-18s: Spanish law treats anyone aged 17 or under as a minor. An unaccompanied minor who comes to the attention of the authorities — after an incident, or in hospital — can be placed in a children's centre until a parent or guardian is found.

Cash and food: If you carry €10,000 or more in cash or travellers' cheques, declare it on arrival and keep the certified declaration — undeclared money can be seized when you leave. You cannot bring meat, milk or products containing them into the EU (powdered baby milk, baby food and medically necessary foods are exceptions), so finish the ham sandwich before you land.

Gibraltar: Since the UK–EU agreement on Gibraltar began to apply provisionally on 15 July 2026, there are no longer entry and exit check booths at the land border between Gibraltar and Spain. Gibraltar's own short-stay entry conditions are now aligned with Schengen, so check them if Gibraltar is on your route.

Working and staying longer: Entering as a tourist does not give you the right to work. Paid work, volunteering placements and long-stay arrangements fall under different rules — see the digital nomads advice. If you genuinely cannot leave before your 90 days run out (a medical emergency, say), you must apply to the immigration authorities (Extranjería) for an extension; the national information line, 060, has English-speaking operators.

2. WHEN TO GO

Spain is several climates stacked together, and the best month depends on where you are standing.

Spring (April–June) and autumn (September–October) are the sweet spots for city-hopping, hiking and the Camino: warm days, manageable crowds, and hostel prices that haven't peaked. September is also when the Mediterranean is at or near its warmest.

Summer (July–August) brings extreme heat to the interior and the south — Seville, Córdoba, Madrid, Extremadura — where afternoon temperatures regularly pass 40°C and the sensible rhythm is to sightsee early, retreat indoors from about midday and come back out in the evening. The coasts are at their most crowded and expensive, hostels sell out, and August is when many Spaniards take their own holidays. Wildfires are a regular summer event across the country, including the islands.

Autumn storms: Heavy rain can cause sudden flash flooding, particularly on the Mediterranean side, with the risk rising from September through the autumn. The deadly floods around Valencia in October 2024 are the reminder. Check the national weather agency's warnings (see the Safety section) before driving or hiking anywhere near a dry riverbed.

Winter (November–March): The high central plateau — Madrid and the Castilian heartland — is properly cold, with frosts. The south coast and the Canary Islands stay mild and are busy with winter sun-seekers. The Sierra Nevada (near Granada) and the Pyrenees are the main ski areas. Green, Atlantic Spain — Galicia, Asturias, Cantabria and the Basque Country — is wet at any time of year, so pack for rain even in summer.

Book ahead for fiestas: Holy Week (Semana Santa), Valencia's Las Fallas and Pamplona's San Fermín are among the events that fill every bed in town months ahead — see our fiestas and festivals advice for how to plan around them. Public holidays also vary by region and town, and shops and some transport run reduced services on them.

3. INSURANCE AND HEALTH CARDS

Travel insurance is non-negotiable. A UK Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC) or European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) gives access to medically necessary state healthcare in Spain on the same basis as residents. It is not insurance: it does not cover repatriation, changes to bookings, non-urgent treatment or any private hospital — and the card is not accepted in private hospitals. In some tourist areas there is no public facility nearby, and hotels sometimes call a private doctor or ambulance on your behalf — which your card will not cover. Buy proper cover as well.

Check the activity list: Standard policies often exclude or restrict skiing and snowboarding (especially off-piste), canyoning, climbing, scuba diving, kitesurfing, paragliding, and riding hired scooters and motorbikes. If you plan any of these, confirm cover before you book. In Spain you can ride a motorcycle up to 125cc from age 16 and need to be 18 for a car or anything larger, and you must carry a valid licence and ID — an insurer is likely to refuse a claim if you weren't licensed for the vehicle.

Mountain rescue can be billed: Catalonia has started billing climbers, skiers and other adventurers whose negligence leads to a rescue. Make sure your policy covers helicopter rescue and medical evacuation.

Balconies and alcohol: Serious and sometimes fatal falls from balconies involve visitors every year. Hotels may fine or evict guests who behave dangerously on balconies, and insurers can refuse claims where drink or drugs were involved. Read your policy's alcohol clause before you assume you are covered for a night out.

Theft cover: Make sure the per-item limit reflects what you actually carry (phone, laptop, camera), and store serial numbers and receipts in the cloud before you leave. Your insurer will ask for a police report (denuncia) for any theft claim — see the Safety section for how to get one.

Save the helpline: If you are referred for hospital treatment, contact your insurer or assistance company quickly. Put its 24-hour number in your phone and on paper.

4. ACCOMMODATION AND BOOKING

Know your words: A hostal is not a hostel — it is a budget hotel, often a good-value private room above a bar or in a town-centre building. A pensión is similar, simpler and often cheaper. An albergue can be a youth hostel or, along the Camino de Santiago, a pilgrim hostel (see the Camino advice). A refugio is a mountain hut. A casa rural is a countryside guesthouse. A parador is a state-run hotel in a historic building — lovely, and not a budget option. Searching "hostel" will find backpacker dorms; searching "hostal" will find you a private room.

Your passport will be registered: Hotels, hostels and other tourist accommodation — and car-hire companies — are legally required to record your passport details when you check in. Wait until staff have scanned or copied it, and don't leave it at reception to collect later. You will need your actual passport on arrival, not just a photo.

Tourist taxes: Several regions and cities add a per-person, per-night tourist tax, usually collected at check-in or checkout and not always included in the booking price. It is a few euros a night in most places and highest in Barcelona, where hostel guests currently pay roughly €6 per person per night in total, with the municipal surcharge scheduled to rise each year. Rates vary by accommodation type and season, so check the property's page.

Book the big sights: The Alhambra in Granada and the Sagrada Família in Barcelona operate timed-entry ticketing and sell out well ahead in busy periods. Buy tickets on the official sites, not from resellers, as soon as your dates are fixed. Walk-up tickets for the most popular attractions cannot be relied on.

Book your first and last nights; leave the middle loose. Outside the main fiestas and the peak weeks of July and August, a flexible itinerary works well in Spain's well-stocked hostel network. Email or message hostels directly for private rooms and longer stays — the best rates and the useful local knowledge often come that way.

5. GETTING THERE

By air: The main international gateways are Madrid, Barcelona, Málaga, Palma, Alicante, Valencia, Seville and Bilbao, with budget airlines flying to many smaller airports as well. Cheap fares come with strict baggage rules — check your airline's cabin-bag dimensions and weight limits, because gate fees can cost more than the ticket. If you are hopping around Spain, consider flying into one city and out of another (an open-jaw ticket) rather than doubling back.

By land and sea: High-speed trains link France with Barcelona, and Portugal is connected by rail and coach. Ferries run between southern Spain and Morocco (Tarifa and Algeciras to Tangier and Ceuta), and Brittany Ferries sails from the UK to Santander and Bilbao. Overland and sea arrivals still involve border checks, and the same Schengen rules apply.

Time zone and power: Mainland Spain and the Balearics are on Central European Time (UTC+1, UTC+2 in summer); the Canary Islands are an hour behind. Sockets are the two-pin European type (types C and F) at 230 V, so UK and US travellers need an adaptor.

6. PHONE, DATA AND CONNECTIVITY

Check roaming before you leave: Roaming terms differ by country and provider, and by whether you are using a plan bought in the EU or outside it. Check your provider's current rates for Spain before you rely on your home SIM.

Buy local or go eSIM: Spain's three main networks are Movistar, Vodafone and Orange, with cheaper brands on the same infrastructure. Expect to show your passport to buy a physical prepaid SIM. An eSIM (Airalo is one provider) lets you land with data already working. Either is far cheaper than roaming for a long trip. WhatsApp is the default way of communicating with hostels, hosts and tour operators.

Download offline maps of the regions you'll visit before you go — hiking, rural bus stops and the Camino are all places where signal drops out.

7. PACKING

Small items that earn their space: A padlock for hostel lockers (many provide the locker, few provide the lock); earplugs, because Spain runs late and hostel dorms in party cities rarely sleep before the small hours; a refillable water bottle; a microfibre towel; sun cream, a hat and sunglasses; insect repellent; and a lightweight scarf or shawl for churches and cathedrals, many of which expect covered shoulders and knees.

Clothing: Pack layers. A summer day in Seville and a February night in Ávila are different planets. Bring one proper waterproof for the north. Walking shoes should be broken in; the rocky coasts also call for water shoes — sea urchins live on many of them. Remember the local dress rule: in parts of Spain it is illegal to walk through towns in just a bikini or swim shorts, or bare-chested, and you can be fined on seafront promenades and adjacent streets.

Documents and medicines: Bring your prescription medicines in their original packaging, plus a copy of the prescription and a letter from your doctor. The legal status of some UK-prescribed medicines can differ in Spain, so check before you travel.

8. PRINT AND SAVE THE IMPORTANT THINGS

Save to your phone and keep on paper: a photo of your passport's photo page, your insurance policy number and 24-hour helpline, your GHIC or EHIC, your first night's address, your return ticket, your bank's emergency numbers, and your embassy's contact details (see the Embassies section). Keep the paper copies separate from the originals. If your phone is stolen, the printout is how you get back on your feet.

9. IF YOU PLAN TO DRIVE

Licence: UK photocard licences are valid for driving in Spain; a UK provisional licence is not, and an old paper licence may need to be updated to a photocard first. If your licence was issued outside the UK, ask the hire company or the Spanish consulate whether you also need an International Driving Permit. Carry your licence and ID whenever you drive.

Rules that catch visitors out: Using a phone while driving is illegal even if you've pulled over to the side of the road, and earpieces are banned too — only fully hands-free systems are allowed. Police run regular roadside checks for alcohol and drugs. Fines can be issued on the spot, and if you accept and pay within 20 days the amount is cut by 50%. Many cities now have low-emission zones (ZBEs): foreign-registered vehicles don't get the Spanish air-pollution sticker, and some cities, including Barcelona, require you to register your vehicle in advance. For more, see transport in the in-country advice.

Staying Safe & Travelling Well

Spain is a safe country to backpack. Violent crime against visitors is rare, and the risks that actually hurt travellers are the dull ones: theft, heat, the sea, the road, and a night out that goes too far. Almost all of it is avoidable if you know what to look for. Read this section once properly and the rest of the trip is mostly just enjoying yourself.

Read More

THE BIG PICTURE

Every major foreign ministry says the same thing about Spain: petty theft is common, serious violence is rare. Pickpocketing and bag-snatching concentrate in big cities — Madrid and Barcelona above all — and in crowded places: public transport, beaches, markets, major monuments and airports. Car break-ins are frequent. Sexual assaults and violent attacks on visitors are uncommon but do happen, so the sensible precautions below apply to everyone.

PICKPOCKETS AND DISTRACTION THEFT

Thieves in Spain are professionals. They use distraction techniques and often work in teams — one person engages you while another lifts your bag or phone. They are especially active when you are encumbered: collecting or checking in luggage at the airport, arranging car hire, and boarding a packed metro with a rucksack on your back.

What works:

Don't keep all your valuables in one place. Split cash, cards and phone between pockets and a hidden pouch; carry one card, not your whole wallet, on a night out. Keep a copy of your passport's photo page somewhere safe.

Phone out of sight near the kerb and the café table. Phones are what most thieves are after. Don't leave one on a table or bar, and don't stand at a kerb or the open door of a metro carriage with it in your hand.

Wear your bag in front, zipped, and keep a hand on it in crowds and on trains. Never hang it from a chair back or leave it under a table at a pavement café. On the beach, take only what you need into the water, or swim in shifts.

Treat anyone who touches you, spills something on you, or hands you something as a pickpocket until proven otherwise. Step away and check your pockets.

Hostel lockers: Use them for your passport, spare cash and electronics, with your own padlock. Make sure accommodation is secure — keep doors and windows locked, and if you're worried about the security of a place, speak to the owner or move.

Cars: Never leave anything visible inside a parked car, and don't leave luggage in one overnight. Foreign-registered and hire cars are known targets.

SCAMS TO KNOW

Fake police: Thieves posing as police officers may ask to see your wallet, claiming they need it "for identification". Genuine officers will ask to see ID but never ask for your wallet or purse, and all police, including those in plain clothes, carry official ID. If you're unsure, ask to go to a police station.

"Highway pirates": Criminals target foreign-registered and hire cars — especially those towing caravans — by flagging you down to say something is wrong with your car or that you've damaged theirs. If you decide to stop, do so only in a lit public place such as a service station, and be wary of anyone offering help. Police in unmarked cars should display a flashing electronic sign reading "Policía" or "Guardia Civil" on the rear window, and genuine police will only ask to see your vehicle documents.

The classics: a stranger who presses a sprig of rosemary or a friendship bracelet on you and then demands payment; a gold ring "found" on the pavement near you; clipboard "petitions" that are cover for a pickpocket working your bag; the "helpful" local at a ticket machine or cash machine who has noticed you're confused; someone telling you a bar or club is "the only place still open" and steering you inside, where an inflated bill awaits. Say no, keep walking, and keep your hand on your things.

Taxis: Only use official, licensed taxis or reputable booked transfers. Licensing rules differ between cities and some require pre-booking. Passengers caught using unlicensed taxi services are liable for fines of up to €600.

Timeshares and "holiday clubs": Be cautious if you are approached on a resort street with a free gift, a scratch card or a "survey" — false incentives are a standard timeshare tactic. Don't sign anything on the spot, and read the timeshare-fraud guidance from your own government before buying or selling one.

Money: Only change money at banks or official exchange offices — unofficial money-changers can give you counterfeit notes, and having or using counterfeit currency is a serious crime in Spain.

NIGHTS OUT

Drink spiking: Be alert to date-rape and other drugs, including GHB and liquid ecstasy. Buy your own drinks, keep them in sight, and don't accept drinks from strangers. Note that drinks served in Spanish bars are often considerably stronger than those in the UK, so a "normal" night's drinking can hit harder than you expect.

Stay with your group: Avoid splitting up from friends, and don't go off with people you've just met. Save the location of your accommodation on your maps app so you can find it when you're tired, and charge your phone before you head out.

Balconies and heights: There have been numerous serious accidents, some fatal, involving visitors falling from balconies and other high places — usually with alcohol or drugs involved. Don't sit on balcony rails and watch out for friends who are at risk. In some regions you can be fined or evicted, and insurance may not cover you.

Illegal parties: In Ibiza and Mallorca, commercially promoted parties in private villas have led to serious accidents. Licensed clubs must meet safety standards for fire exits, capacity limits and trained security; illegal parties needn't. You can also be fined for attending.

Festivals and processions: Large events and public processions happen throughout the year. Follow police and local-authority instructions, know where the exits are, and be extra careful of your valuables in the crush.

WOMEN, SOLO AND LGBTQ+ TRAVELLERS

Women and solo travellers: Spain is a comfortable country for women travelling alone and for solo travellers generally. The usual rules apply with extra force at night: watch your drink, stay with people you trust, and use a licensed taxi or booked ride rather than walking a long way alone. Hostels are good places to find companions for evenings out. If you have a problem, 016 is Spain's free, confidential, 24-hour helpline for violence against women, including sexual violence, with interpreters in more than 50 languages; the call doesn't appear on your phone bill. It can also be reached on WhatsApp at 600 000 016. In an emergency, call 112.

LGBTQ+ travellers: Spain is generally tolerant and progressive. There are active LGBTQ+ communities and social venues, particularly in big cities. Same-sex marriage is legal, and transgender people can register under their preferred sex in public documents without prior surgery. Take the usual precautions in any city, and see the Medical section for current sexual-health advice.

POLICE, ID AND REPORTING A CRIME

You must identify yourself to police. If a police officer asks for photo ID — Guardia Civil, national, regional or local police — you must provide it. Police can hold you at a station until your identity is confirmed, and ignoring a direct request can count as "disobedience", which is a criminal offence. It is also why you shouldn't leave your passport at hotel reception.

The passport dilemma: Governments including the US, Canada and Australia advise carrying a copy or photo of your passport's photo page rather than the original, because passports are a prize for thieves. Whether a copy is accepted is up to the officer: the legal requirement is to prove who you are, and the UK government warns that police can hold you at a station until they have confirmed it. A sensible approach is to carry a clear copy and a second photo ID if you have one, keep the original locked up in the busiest pickpocket cities, and carry the original when you travel between towns or when you're in remote areas.

If you are robbed:

1. If a crime is happening now or someone is in danger, call 112 (or 091, the national police).

2. Get a denuncia — an official police report. You need it for any insurance claim. You can report in person at any Policía Nacional station, and in many tourist cities at a SATE (Servicio de Atención al Turista Extranjero), a police office for foreign visitors with interpreters. The Policía Nacional also runs a phone reporting line for tourists on 902 102 112 (from within Spain), with English among the languages offered, and an online form at denuncias.policia.es in English, French, German and Italian. Phone and online reports must be signed and confirmed in person at a police station within 48 hours, and online reporting isn't available for violent or sexual crimes or where the offender is known.

3. If your passport is stolen, contact your embassy for an emergency travel document. A police report is not a substitute for a passport at the border — you cannot use one to leave Spain.

4. Cancel your cards immediately and contact your insurer.

AlertCops: The Policía Nacional and Guardia Civil run an official free app, AlertCops, for reporting incidents or calling for help discreetly, including a panic button that sends your location and a short audio clip. The app works in several languages, including English, and has live chat translation in more than 100. Install it and set it up before you travel.

HEAT, FIRE AND FLOOD

Extreme heat: Summer heat can be dangerous, and it is the biggest weather risk most backpackers face. Check warnings from AEMET, Spain's meteorological agency (aemet.es), and from Meteoalarm. The Spanish Ministry of Health also runs a free heat-health alert service by email (in Spanish) for your area. Plan hikes and sightseeing for early morning and evening, carry more water than you think you need, wear a hat and high-factor sun cream, and don't be proud: if you feel dizzy, nauseous or stop sweating, get into shade and seek help.

Wildfires: Wildfires happen often in summer, including on the islands. Causing one — even unintentionally — is a criminal offence in Spain. Stub out cigarettes properly, never light barbecues or campfires in or near woodland (fines can be heavy), and don't leave glass bottles behind. If you see a fire, call 112 straight away. If you are told to evacuate, go; conditions can change fast. AEMET publishes a wildfire-risk forecast.

Flash floods: Check AEMET's warnings before driving or hiking, especially in autumn, and stay out of dry riverbeds, underpasses and low-lying roads when heavy rain is forecast. If your phone sounds a loud emergency alert, read it (use a translation app) and follow it — it is Spain's public warning system.

BEACHES AND THE SEA

People drown in the sea and in pools in Spain every year. Learn the beach flag system: a red flag means you must not enter the water. Take extra care and ask locally if a beach has no lifeguards, flags or signs. Some beaches, especially around the islands, have strong undercurrents. Waves on unmonitored beaches can come in much further than you expect, with strong undertow. Don't dive into unfamiliar water — hidden rocks and shallow depths cause serious injuries — and don't swim where a river meets the sea. Follow local advice when jellyfish are present.

Also: public sex and heavy drinking on beaches are regulated, and in some places you can be fined for being on the seafront in just swimwear or bare-chested (see the Drinking & Nightlife Laws section).

MOUNTAINS AND HIKING

Temperatures in the mountains can change very quickly. Check the local forecast, including warnings of extreme heat or cold, before every walk. Altitude sickness is a risk in some of the higher ranges, particularly in the Granada, Huesca and Tenerife areas — see the Medical section. Tell someone your route and expected return time, carry a charged phone and an offline map, and pack more clothing than the valley weather suggests.

If you get into trouble in the mountains or become lost, call 112 or the Guardia Civil on 062. Catalonia has started billing climbers, skiers and other adventurers whose negligence leads to a rescue — another reason to check that your insurance covers rescue. For avalanche conditions in ski areas, consult the European Avalanche Warning Services.

POWER CUTS AND DISRUPTION

On 28 April 2025, a power failure shut down electricity across the Iberian Peninsula for hours. Trains and metros stopped, traffic lights went dark, mobile networks and cash machines failed, and shops could only take cash. It was an exceptional event, but it is the reason for a simple habit: carry a small reserve of cash, a charged power bank, and offline maps, and write down your accommodation's address and phone number on paper.

TERRORISM, PROTESTS AND STRIKES

Terrorism: The UK government assesses that terrorists are likely to try to carry out attacks in Spain, which could be indiscriminate and could affect places visited by foreign nationals. This is a statement of risk, not a reason to stay away: be aware of your surroundings, follow local media and heed the instructions of the authorities.

Demonstrations: Protests, political gatherings and marches can happen with little or no warning, particularly in cities. Most are peaceful, but if you see signs of disorder, move away. Avoid demonstrations altogether.

Strikes: Check with your airline, tour operator or transport company for planned strikes before you travel, and leave slack in your itinerary around important connections.

Money & Budgeting

Spain is one of the better-value countries in Western Europe, and a backpacker can eat very well on very little — provided you learn how the system works. The set-price lunch, the supermarket breakfast and the free-tapa bar will do more for your budget than any amount of hostel haggling. The traps are the usual ones: bad cash-machine fees, being charged in the wrong currency, and city prices at the top of the range.

CURRENCY, CARDS AND CASH

The euro (€): Spain uses the euro. Contactless cards and phone payments are accepted almost everywhere in cities and towns, and you can often get through a whole trip on a card. Cash still matters in small bars, village shops, markets, some hostels and the occasional taxi, and — as the blackout of April 2025 showed, when card machines failed and shops took cash only — it is the fallback when technology isn't working. Keep a small reserve.

Carry two cards from different providers (for instance a Wise or Revolut card alongside your normal bank card), stored in different places. Tell your bank your travel dates so it doesn't freeze the card on the first day.

Always pay in euros. If a card terminal or cash machine offers to charge you in your home currency ("dynamic currency conversion"), decline it and choose euros. The rate offered is almost always worse than your own bank's.

Locals' payment apps: You will see Spaniards splitting bills and paying friends with Bizum. It is tied to Spanish bank accounts, so don't expect to use it as a visitor.

CASH MACHINES (ATMs)

Withdrawing cash abroad is where many travellers lose money without noticing. In Spain, cash machines run by banks commonly charge foreign cards a fee on top of anything your own bank adds, and standalone machines on tourist streets (you will see Euronet among others) tend to charge the most and push the home-currency conversion described above.

Check the screen before you confirm: The machine should show you the fee before the transaction goes through. If the fee is high, cancel and use another machine — the difference between a fee-free card and a bad machine can be several euros per withdrawal. Withdraw larger amounts less often rather than small amounts frequently, subject to your daily limits and to the pickpocketing risks in the Safety section. Make withdrawals inside bank branches or in daylight, shield the keypad, and never accept "help" from a stranger at a machine.

Cash from home: Don't rely on airport or street currency-exchange booths for the bulk of your money; they generally offer poor rates. If you do change cash, use a bank or official exchange office. Remember the declaration rule if you carry €10,000 or more.

WHAT THINGS COST

Prices below are planning ranges for 2026, not tariffs: they vary by city, season and neighbourhood, and the big tourist centres sit at the top.

Dorm beds: Roughly €18–€40 a night, with Madrid, Barcelona and the coasts in high season at the upper end and smaller towns and the interior at the lower end. Add the tourist tax where it applies (see the Preflight Checklists).

The menú del día: This is the backpacker's best friend. A weekday set lunch of a starter, main course, dessert or coffee, bread and a drink, usually served from around 13:30 to 15:30, typically costs between roughly €11 and €20 — near the bottom of that range in provincial towns, and towards the top in central Madrid, Barcelona, Bilbao and San Sebastián. Make lunch your main meal and eat lightly in the evening; it is the single biggest saving you can make.

Coffee, beer and tapas: A coffee at the bar is usually around €1.50 to €3. A caña (a small draught beer) is typically €2 to €3. Individual tapas are mostly €2 to €5 each. In Granada and parts of Andalusia, many bars bring a free tapa with every drink, which can make a round or two cover dinner. Drinking at the bar is often cheaper than sitting on a terrace, where prices can be higher.

Supermarkets: Chains such as Mercadona, Lidl, Carrefour and Dia are cheap and good. Self-catering breakfast and a picnic lunch keeps costs down, and most hostels have a kitchen.

Sights: Many state museums and monuments have free-entry hours or free days for everybody or for certain age groups. Check each website before you pay full price. Pre-book the popular sights rather than paying more on the day, or being turned away.

A realistic daily budget: For a backpacker sleeping in a dorm, eating a set lunch with a light supermarket-based breakfast and dinner, using local transport and paying for one sight, think roughly €45–€75 per person per day, nearer the low end in smaller inland towns and the high end in Madrid, Barcelona, and the islands in summer. This is our planning range built from the prices above, not a quoted tariff. Add intercity trains and buses, guided tours, activities, and nights out; they add up faster than the food does.

TIPPING AND EVERYDAY HABITS

Tipping: It is not obligatory in Spain. Locals mostly don't tip for a coffee or a single beer, and menu prices include tax. In a sit-down restaurant with good service, rounding up or leaving a few euros is appreciated, but nobody expects 15–20%. The same goes for taxis: round up.

Haggling isn't customary in shops or markets, with the exception of the occasional street-market stall selling souvenirs.

Share costs openly: Hostels are the best way to find travel partners to split fares, car-hire costs and the cost of a rural taxi. A shared-expenses app such as Splitwise keeps things fair.

MONEY MISTAKES TO AVOID

Paying a stranger "on the spot": Unlicensed taxis, "free" bracelets and unofficial guides all end in a demand for money. Use licensed taxis and book tours through your hostel or an established operator.

Unchecked bills: In busy tourist areas, check the bill carefully. Look for items you didn't order or prices that don't match the menu, and ask for the bill (la cuenta) itemised if you need to. Check what a drink costs before you order in a bar you've been steered to.

Counterfeit notes: Only change money at banks or official exchange offices. Counterfeit currency is a serious crime in Spain, including if you pass it on unknowingly.

Medical

Spain has a good healthcare system, there is no malaria, and no vaccine certificate is required to enter. What actually puts backpackers in a clinic is heat, sun, alcohol, a fall, an infected bite or a sports injury. This section covers how to get help, what to vaccinate for, the insect-borne diseases currently being reported, and sexual health. If you take regular medication or have a long-term condition, speak to your doctor before you go.

Read More

BEFORE YOU GO

See a travel-health professional: Ideally at least eight weeks before departure, and certainly four to six, so there is time for any vaccine course. For UK travellers, TravelHealthPro (travelhealthpro.org.uk) publishes the current Spain advice and live outbreak notices, and the NHS travel-vaccinations page explains what is available and what it costs.

Vaccinations: There are no certificate requirements for entering Spain. Make sure your routine vaccinations are up to date — measles/mumps/rubella and diphtheria–tetanus–polio. A tetanus booster is recommended if your last dose was more than ten years ago, even if you've had a full course. Beyond that, nothing is routinely recommended for most travellers; your clinic will advise on anything specific to your trip.

Rabies: Rabies has not been reported in domestic or wild animals in mainland Spain, the Balearic Islands or the Canary Islands, so most travellers are at low risk. The exceptions are the North African territories of Ceuta and Melilla, where there is a risk and you should avoid contact with animals. Bats everywhere can carry bat lyssavirus, which is preventable with prompt treatment. Don't handle bats, and if you are bitten or scratched by any animal, clean the wound thoroughly and seek local medical assessment immediately, even if the injury looks trivial.

Medicines: Carry prescription medicines in their original packaging, with a copy of the prescription and a letter from your doctor. The legal status of some medicines prescribed or bought in the UK, and in other countries, can differ in Spain. Check the rules for yours before you go, particularly for controlled drugs such as strong painkillers, sedatives and stimulants, and carry the doctor's letter with them. Pack enough for the whole trip plus a few days' margin.

GETTING HELP IN SPAIN

In an emergency, call 112 and ask for an ambulance. Operators can handle calls in many languages, including English. Contact your insurer or assistance company quickly if you are referred for treatment.

Public versus private care: The public system provides emergency care at hospital emergency departments (urgencias), and routine care at health centres (centros de salud). If you hold a UK GHIC or an EHIC, it gives access to medically necessary state-provided care on the same terms as residents; it does not cover private hospitals, repatriation or non-urgent care. If you need hospital treatment, you're more likely to receive appropriate care in a public facility, though in some tourist areas there may not be one nearby. Some hotels call private doctors or ambulances, to which your health card does not apply — always ask where you're being taken, and tell your insurer. Visitors without a European card should expect to pay and claim back; keep every receipt and report.

Pharmacies (farmacias, marked with a green cross) are the first stop for minor problems: pharmacists can advise on cuts, stings, stomach upsets and minor infections, and sell many medicines over the counter. Pharmacies take turns to stay open overnight and on holidays (farmacia de guardia), and the rota is posted on the door of every pharmacy. In tourist areas staff often speak English.

Respiratory infections: During flu and COVID-19 season, you may be required to wear a face mask in doctors' surgeries, hospitals and pharmacies. Rules vary by region, so follow local instructions. Seasonal flu in the northern hemisphere runs from as early as October through to March.

INSECT-BORNE DISEASES: WHAT'S BEING REPORTED

There is no malaria in Spain. The insect- and tick-borne diseases are uncommon, and none can be prevented with a vaccine or tablets: the only protection is avoiding bites, day and night. The figures below are the latest published by UK health authorities as of October 2026; for current numbers, check TravelHealthPro's outbreak page.

West Nile virus: Spread by mosquitoes; 104 locally acquired cases had been reported in Spain across five areas as of 10 September 2026. Most infections cause no symptoms or a mild flu-like illness; a small number are serious.

Dengue: The mosquitoes that spread dengue feed during the day and are more common in towns and cities. Locally acquired dengue has been reported in Spain since 2018, in the provinces of Cádiz, Catalonia and Murcia and on the island of Ibiza. Vaccination isn't recommended for visitors; avoid daytime mosquito bites.

Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF): A tick-borne virus. Four locally acquired cases had been reported in Spain in 2026 as of mid-August, three of them in the Salamanca area. Cases are rare, but walkers and campers in rural areas of western Spain should take tick precautions seriously.

Leishmaniasis: Spread by sandflies in southern Europe, including Spain. Use repellent and cover up at dusk in rural areas.

How to protect yourself: Use an insect repellent on exposed skin (DEET-based repellents are the standard recommendation), wear long sleeves and trousers at dusk and when walking through long grass or scrub, sleep under a net or screen where windows are open, and check your skin and clothing for ticks after a walk. If you find a tick, remove it promptly and properly with fine tweezers or a tick-removal tool, and seek advice if you develop a fever or rash.

HEAT, SUN AND DEHYDRATION

Heat is the commonest serious health risk for travellers in Spain in summer. Drink water regularly (tap water is safe to drink in most of mainland Spain — see the note below), do hard exercise early in the morning or in the evening rather than in the middle of the day, wear a hat and use high-factor sun cream, and take it seriously when alcohol, dehydration and 40°C meet. Signs of heat exhaustion include heavy sweating, headache, dizziness, nausea and cramps; heatstroke — confusion, hot dry skin, a very high temperature — is a medical emergency, so call 112. Check the Spanish heat-health warnings (see Safety).

ALTITUDE

Altitude illness is a risk at 2,500 metres and above. In Spain that means the Sierra Nevada (Mulhacén, 3,479 m), Teide on Tenerife (3,718 m), and parts of the Pyrenees. Rapid ascent is the cause. Spend a few days below 3,000 m before going higher; avoid raising your sleeping altitude by more than 500 m a day once you're above 3,000 m, and take a rest day every three or four days. Headache, fatigue, loss of appetite, nausea and poor sleep are signs of acute mountain sickness — stop going up, and descend if you aren't improving. Severe breathlessness, confusion or loss of coordination are emergencies that need immediate descent and medical help.

FOOD, WATER AND THE SEA

Food and water: Spain isn't a high-risk country for food- and water-borne illness, and the usual common-sense hygiene applies. Tap water is safe to drink across most of mainland Spain, though it can taste strongly of minerals or chlorine in some places, particularly on the islands and parts of the Mediterranean coast, where many locals drink bottled water. Don't drink untreated water from mountain streams.

Sea hazards: Jellyfish occur on Spanish coasts, and local authorities often post warnings; follow local advice. Wear water shoes on rocky shores to avoid sea urchin spines. Ask a lifeguard or pharmacist how to treat a sting, and seek medical help for anything severe.

SEXUAL HEALTH

Anyone who is sexually active is at risk of sexually transmitted infections wherever they are. The risk goes up with casual or new partners, sex without a condom, and sex under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Condoms are sold in pharmacies, supermarkets and vending machines, and emergency contraception can be bought in a pharmacy.

Vaccines to check before you go: UK health authorities advise that, where eligible, you should be up to date with the vaccines that protect against hepatitis B, HPV, mpox and gonorrhoea (the MenB vaccine), and to consider HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) if appropriate to you.

What's currently being reported: Mpox clade Ib has been reported in Spain — 82 cases by mid-February 2026, most of them among gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men — and UK health authorities advise extra precautions. A bacterial skin infection, dermatophilosis, has also produced a cluster in Spain: 23 confirmed cases between July 2025 and July 2026, mostly linked to sex-on-premises venues.

After a possible exposure: If you think you've been exposed to HIV, go to a hospital emergency department (urgencias) as soon as possible — post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) only works if started within 72 hours. See a doctor and mention your travel history if you develop any symptoms, and get tested after returning home if you had sex without a condom with a new partner while abroad. Testing in the UK is free and confidential.

MENTAL HEALTH

Long trips can be lonely and tiring, and alcohol and sleep disruption in hostels don't help. If you have a mental-health condition, plan how you'll access support and medication on the road; the UK government and TravelHealthPro both publish guidance on travelling with mental-health conditions. In a crisis, call 112.

IF YOU GET SICK OR INJURED

1. For anything life-threatening, call 112.

2. For anything else, go to a pharmacy or a health centre, or to the emergency department at a public hospital, and show your GHIC or EHIC if you have one.

3. Phone your insurer's 24-hour line before agreeing to expensive treatment, especially at a private clinic.

4. Keep every receipt, prescription and medical report.

5. Tell someone at your hostel where you're going.

Embassies

Nearly all embassies are in Madrid. Some countries also run consulates in other cities — Barcelona above all — which handle day-to-day services for their citizens, such as emergency travel documents. If your passport is stolen, report it to the police first (see the Safety section), then contact your embassy: a police report will not get you across a border, but an emergency travel document will. Many embassies work by appointment only, so phone or use the website before you turn up.

Read More

WHAT YOUR EMBASSY CAN AND CAN'T DO

An embassy or consulate can issue emergency travel documents, help you contact family, give you a list of local lawyers and doctors, and support you if you are the victim of a serious crime, are arrested or become seriously ill. It cannot pay your bills, get you out of prison, or interfere in the Spanish legal process. If you are arrested, you have the right to a lawyer and an interpreter and to have your consulate informed — ask for all three.

Register or subscribe to your government's travel-alert service if it has one, and read its Spain travel advice before you go: UK (FCDO), USA (State Department), Canada, Australia (Smartraveller).

Drinking & Nightlife Laws

Spain is relaxed about drinking and famously late-running at night — and, increasingly, strict about what that looks like in public. Street drinking, drugs, beach behaviour, swimwear in town centres and a handful of resort-specific bans are all enforced, often with on-the-spot fines. The rules are set by regions and individual councils, so what is shrugged at in one town can be fined in the next. Know the basics and you can enjoy one of Europe's best nights out without a surprise bill.

Read More

ALCOHOL: THE BASICS

The legal age: You must be 18 to buy or drink alcohol in Spain. Carry photo ID — bars, clubs and shops can and do check, and you must in any case produce ID if the police ask.

Drinking in the street: It is illegal to drink alcohol in the street in many parts of Spain, and in most cities outdoor drinking is allowed only at licensed bars and cafés with terraces. This is the botellón — the Spanish tradition of drinking in a park or plaza before going out — and it is banned by local ordinances in many towns. Police can issue on-the-spot fines; amounts vary widely by municipality, from around €100 up to well over €1,000 in some cities. A bottle from the supermarket is not worth it. Drink at a bar, or drink in your hostel.

Strong drinks: Drinks served in Spanish bars are often stronger than those in the UK. Spirit measures tend to be generous, and mixed drinks (copas, cubatas) are often free-poured. Pace yourself accordingly, particularly when the nights are long.

Late-night shop sales: Some regions and cities restrict late-night takeaway alcohol sales; the Balearic resort rules below are one example. If a shop refuses to sell you a drink late at night, this is probably why.

THE BALEARIC "EXCESS" RULES

Local laws in some party resorts limit how alcohol can be sold. In Magaluf (Calvià) and Playa de Palma on Mallorca, and in Sant Antoni de Portmany (San Antonio) on Ibiza, the following are banned:

— happy hours and open bars ("all you can drink in one hour" offers);
— alcohol from vending machines and self-service dispensers;
— organising pub crawls and party-boat trips; and
— "off-licence" sales between 9:30 p.m. and 8 a.m.

Hotels and other places will evict you if you behave dangerously on balconies, and you could also be fined.

NIGHTLIFE: HOW IT WORKS

Spanish nights start late. Dinner is commonly at 9 or 10 p.m., bars fill up afterwards, and clubs typically don't get busy until well after midnight, often running until the early morning. Plan your energy — and your hostel's quiet hours — around it. If you want to sleep before 3 a.m. in a party-city hostel, ask for a dorm on a quiet floor or bring earplugs.

Clubs: Entry is often cheaper earlier in the night or with a guest-list or flyer from a promoter; many include a drink. Drinks inside cost far more than in a bar. Hostel staff usually know which clubs run guest lists and which to avoid, and some organise pub crawls — outside the banned areas above. Treat any "free drink" offered to you on the street, or any stranger steering you into a bar, with suspicion (see the Safety section on scams and spiking).

Licensed venues versus illegal parties: Licensed clubs and bars have to meet safety standards: emergency exits, capacity limits and trained, licensed security staff. Illegal commercially promoted parties in villas and private homes, particularly in Ibiza and Mallorca, needn't, and attending one can get you fined.

DRUGS, INCLUDING CANNABIS

Be clear about this one: cannabis is not legal in Spain, and foreign-ministry advice, including the UK's, is blunt: illegal drugs, including cannabis, carry severe penalties, and possession of even a small quantity can lead to arrest and detention.

How the law works in practice: Under Spain's public-safety law (the Ley de Seguridad Ciudadana), consuming or possessing drugs in public places — streets, parks, beaches — is an administrative offence with fines starting at €601 and running up to €30,000. Consumption in a genuinely private place is treated differently, but the line between private and public is not one to test: a hostel's common areas, a terrace or a balcony overlooking the street may not count as private, and most hostels ban drug use outright and will evict you. Selling, supplying and trafficking are criminal offences that carry prison sentences, and the UK government warns visitors to expect a long jail sentence and heavy fines for possessing, using or smuggling illegal drugs.

Cannabis clubs: Members-only "cannabis social clubs" operate in a legal grey area. They have been the subject of repeated crackdowns, notably in Barcelona, and their existence is not proof that what happens inside is legal for you. Don't treat them as a tourist attraction.

Street dealers: People who approach tourists to sell drugs in the busy parts of big cities are a risk in themselves: what they sell is often not what they claim it is, and an approach like this can be cover for theft. Don't accept drinks or pills from strangers.

Airports and borders: Spanish airports use excellent detection technology, which is also used to scan the baggage of passengers who are only transiting. Never carry drugs across a border. Expect a long jail sentence and heavy fines for smuggling.

Nitrous oxide: Some areas fine the sale or consumption of nitrous oxide ("laughing gas"), so a balloon at a party is not a legal grey area everywhere.

If someone is in trouble: If a friend collapses or becomes unwell after taking something, call 112 immediately and tell the ambulance crew what they took. Spanish harm-reduction organisations such as Energy Control publish plain-language information about drug risks.

DRINK-DRIVING

Spain has strict drink-driving laws. Police regularly carry out roadside checks for alcohol and drugs, and penalties include fines, loss of your licence and prison. The simple rule for a backpacker: if you've been drinking, don't drive, and don't ride a hired scooter either.

SMOKING AND VAPING

Smoking is banned in enclosed public places, including bars, restaurants and public transport. Outdoor rules vary, and a growing number of local authorities restrict smoking on beaches and in some outdoor areas. Look for signs and follow them.

OTHER RULES THAT CATCH TOURISTS OUT

Public behaviour: There are strict controls on drinking and sexual activity in public places, including on beaches.

Swimwear in town: In some parts of Spain it is illegal to be in the street wearing only a bikini or swimming shorts, and being bare-chested is also illegal in some areas. You can be fined if you are caught in swimwear on the seafront promenade or in the streets behind it. Put a T-shirt or wrap on between the beach and the bar.

Religious dress in public buildings: For security reasons, some public authorities in Spain don't allow the burka or niqab to be worn inside their buildings, and if you visit town council buildings wearing one, you may be asked to remove it while inside.

Wildfire rules: Lighting a barbecue or campfire in or near forest areas can bring a heavy fine, and starting a wildfire — even by accident — is a criminal offence. See the Safety section.

Respect the police: If an officer asks you for ID, hand it over and stay calm; ignoring a direct request can be treated as "disobedience", a criminal offence. If you are detained, ask for a lawyer, an interpreter and your consulate.

Useful Apps & Websites

Download and set up the safety and transport apps before you land — not when you need them. Most of the official sites below have English versions, and a browser translation will fill in the rest.

Read More

SAFETY AND EMERGENCY

AlertCops: The official free app of the Policía Nacional and Guardia Civil. It lets you report a crime or call for help discreetly, with a panic button that sends your location to the police. It works in several languages, including English, and has live chat translation. Install it and set it up before you need it.

112: The single emergency number for police, ambulance and fire across Spain. You can call it free from any phone, including one without a SIM card or credit.

denuncias.policia.es: The Policía Nacional's online form for reporting thefts, in English, French, German and Italian. You must confirm the report in person at a police station within 48 hours. See the Safety section.

AEMET: Spain's national weather agency. Check its warnings for heat, storms, flooding and wildfire risk before every day trip, hike or long drive.

Meteoalarm: European weather warnings in one place, in English.

TravelHealthPro: UK-based, with current health risks and outbreak notices for Spain. Useful for non-UK travellers too.

TRANSPORT AND NAVIGATION

Google Maps offline: Download the regions you'll visit before you go. Signal can be thin in the mountains, on the Camino and in rural interiors.

Renfe, Ouigo and Iryo: The main train operators. Compare all three on the busiest high-speed routes, and book as soon as your plans are firm — the cheapest fares go first. See transport for how to choose.

ALSA and FlixBus: Long-distance coaches. ALSA is Spain's biggest bus company and reaches towns that trains don't.

BlaBlaCar: Ride-sharing between towns. It's popular in Spain and often cheaper than the bus.

Taxi and ride apps: Apps such as FREE NOW and Cabify are widely used for booking licensed rides, and Uber and Bolt also operate in some cities. Availability and rules vary by city — licensing differs across Spain and some cities require pre-booking — so check what operates where you are, and only use licensed vehicles.

Wikiloc and Komoot: Trail maps and user-shared GPS routes for walking, cycling and the Camino. Download routes before you set off.

LANGUAGE

Google Translate: Download Spanish for offline use. The camera mode is excellent for menus, signs and warning notices.

DeepL and SpanishDict: DeepL is often better at natural-sounding sentences; SpanishDict is a good dictionary with verb tables when you want to understand a word rather than just translate it.

MONEY AND PAYMENTS

Wise / Revolut: A travel card alongside your normal bank card is the standard backpacker combination. Always pay in euros when a terminal offers to convert.

XE Currency: Live exchange rates, for checking what you're really paying.

ACCOMMODATION AND BOOKING

Hostelworld and Booking.com: The most widely used booking platforms. Many smaller hostels and hostales aren't listed, so it's worth messaging properties directly for private rooms and long stays.

Official ticket sites: Book the Alhambra and the Sagrada Família through their own websites rather than through resellers.

CONNECTIVITY

Airalo: eSIM provider — install a Spain data eSIM before you fly so that you have data the moment you land. For longer trips, a prepaid SIM from one of Spain's networks (Movistar, Vodafone, Orange or a cheaper brand on their networks) is usually better value; you'll need your passport to buy one.

WhatsApp: The default way to message hostels, hosts, tour operators and fellow travellers in Spain.

FOOD

TheFork: Restaurant booking, with discounts at some venues. Useful for dinner reservations in busy cities.

Too Good To Go: Cheap surprise bags of end-of-day food from bakeries, supermarkets and restaurants. A budget trick in the bigger cities.

OFFICIAL INFORMATION

EU Travel Europe: The official EU site for the Entry/Exit System and ETIAS. Check it before you book, and whenever you hear that ETIAS has started.

Spain.info: Spain's official tourism site. Useful for regional event listings and general trip planning.

Policía Nacional: Official police website, including tourist-safety information and the list of SATE offices.

060: Spain's national information line, with English-speaking operators available. Useful for immigration questions and official procedures.
