Advice For Backpackers

Essential Travel Advice for Backpackers - In-country

Emergencies

If something goes wrong in Spain, the good news is that the system works and it is not complicated: one number reaches everyone, operators speak English, and the emergency services are well organised. The bad news is that most trouble happens somewhere inconvenient — a mountain path, a beach with no signal, a night out you can't remember the address of. Read this section before you need it, save the numbers in your phone, and write the important ones on paper.

Read More

EMERGENCY NUMBERS

112 — everything. The single emergency number for police, ambulance and fire, and the only one you need to remember. It works across Spain and the whole EU, it is free from any phone, and the operators can handle calls in English. Say "Emergency", say where you are, and answer their questions; they will send the right service. Know your location before you call: the street and number, the name of your hostel, a kilometre marker on a road, the name of the beach or trailhead.

If you prefer to go direct, or are told to, these are the others:

091 — Policía Nacional (national police): crime, theft and urgent situations in cities and towns.

092 — Policía Local (municipal police): traffic problems, disturbances and lost property in a town or city.

062 — Guardia Civil: the paramilitary police force that covers rural areas and highways, and also handles mountain rescue and wildlife emergencies.

061 — ambulance and medical emergencies in most regions. Calling 112 gets you the same ambulance, so if in doubt use 112.

Fire: 112 is simplest. Local fire-brigade numbers vary by region (you'll see 080 and 085); for a forest fire, call 112 immediately.

900 202 202 — Salvamento Marítimo (Maritime Rescue): anyone in trouble at sea or on the coast. On a boat with a VHF radio, use channel 16.

016 — violence against women: free, confidential, 24-hour helpline with interpreters in more than 50 languages. The call doesn't show on your phone bill. It can also be reached on WhatsApp at 600 000 016.

024 — suicide prevention: free, confidential and open 24 hours a day.

900 525 100 — Samaritans in Spain: an English-language emotional-support helpline.

060: the national information line for non-emergency official questions, with English-speaking operators available.

WORDS WORTH KNOWING

¡Ayuda! (Help!) · ¡Socorro! (Do you speak English?) · Fuego (Fire) · Estoy herido/a (I'm injured).

MOUNTAIN AND SEA RESCUE

In the mountains: call 112 (or the Guardia Civil on 062) as soon as you realise you are lost, injured or cannot go on — don't wait until dark and your battery are both nearly gone. Give your position (coordinates from your phone's map app are ideal), the number of people, and what is wrong. Stay put unless you are in danger, keep warm and dry, and make yourself visible. Before you set off, tell someone your route and your expected return time. Remember that Catalonia has started billing people whose negligence causes a rescue, so make sure your insurance covers rescue (see the pre-flight advice on insurance).

At the beach: swim where there are lifeguards and flags. A red flag means you must not enter the water. If you're caught in a rip current, don't fight it: stay calm, float or swim parallel to the shore, and wave and shout for help. Don't swim where a river meets the sea. If someone is in trouble, call 112 or 900 202 202 first, and don't become a second casualty by going in after them without something to hold or float on.

Jellyfish and stings: if jellyfish are present, authorities usually post warnings. Ask the lifeguard or a pharmacist how to treat a sting, and seek medical help for anything severe.

IF YOU ARE ROBBED OR ASSAULTED

1. Get safe first. Your phone, bag and passport can be replaced. If anyone is in danger, or the crime is happening now, call 112 (or 091). Don't chase thieves.

2. Get a denuncia (police report). You need it for any insurance claim, and it is the first step to replacing a passport. Go to any Policía Nacional station (comisaría) — or, in many tourist cities, to a SATE office (Servicio de Atención al Turista Extranjero), a police office for foreign visitors with interpreters. Check policia.es for the current list of SATE offices. Ask for a copy and keep it.

3. The phone and online options. The Policía Nacional runs a reporting line for tourists on 902 102 112 (from within Spain), which at last check offered English, French, German, Italian and Spanish, and an online form at denuncias.policia.es. Both must be signed and confirmed in person at a police station within 48 hours, and online reporting isn't available for violent or sexual crimes or when the offender is known.

4. Passport stolen? Contact your embassy (see the Embassies section of the pre-flight advice) for an emergency travel document. A police report will not get you across a border, and you cannot use one to leave Spain.

5. Cards and phone. Freeze or cancel your cards using your own bank's app or number, and contact your phone provider to block the SIM. Tell your insurer. If you hold a second card, now is when you'll be glad it was kept elsewhere.

6. If you are assaulted — including sexually assaulted — call 112, go to a hospital emergency department (urgencias), and tell staff what happened; they can examine you, treat injuries and involve the police. Women can also call 016 at any hour for confidential support in your language. Your embassy can help with hospitals, lawyers and contacting family.

7. If you are stopped or detained by police, stay calm, show photo ID when asked, and don't argue. If you are detained, ask for a lawyer, an interpreter and for your consulate to be told.

MEDICAL EMERGENCIES: PUBLIC VS PRIVATE

Life-threatening: call 112 and ask for an ambulance. The ambulance will take you to the nearest suitable hospital, which will usually be a public one.

Public hospitals have emergency departments (urgencias) that treat everyone. If you hold a UK GHIC or an EHIC, show it: it gives access to medically necessary state-provided care on the same terms as local residents. It will not cover private hospitals, repatriation or non-urgent care, and in some tourist areas there is no public facility nearby. Expect waits at busy emergency departments, and bring your passport and your insurance details.

Private hospitals and clinics are everywhere in the big cities and on the tourist coasts — large groups such as Quirónsalud, Vithas and HM Hospitales run hospitals in many cities. They are quicker and more likely to have English-speaking staff, but you pay, or your insurer pays. Phone your insurer's 24-hour line before agreeing to expensive treatment, and ask them to confirm they will guarantee payment. If a hotel or hostel calls a private doctor or ambulance for you, ask where you're being taken and who pays.

Minor problems: go to a pharmacy (farmacia, green cross). Pharmacies take turns to open overnight and on holidays (farmacia de guardia) and the duty rota is posted on every pharmacy door. For anything that isn't urgent but needs a doctor, ask your insurer to direct you to a clinic.

Keep everything: receipts, prescriptions, discharge reports and test results. Your insurer will need them, and a doctor at home may too.

WEATHER AND DISASTER ALERTS

ES-Alert is Spain's public emergency-warning system: when authorities need to warn people in an affected area — about a fire, flood or other danger — it sends a message directly to phones in that area, with a loud alert. If your phone does this, read the message (use a translation app) and follow it.

AEMET (aemet.es), Spain's weather agency, issues colour-coded warnings for heat, storms, rain and wildfire risk. Check it before every hike, drive or beach day in summer and autumn. If you are told to evacuate because of a wildfire or flood, go immediately; conditions can change faster than you expect.

Power cuts: if the power goes out, mobile networks and card machines can fail with it, as they did across the Iberian Peninsula on 28 April 2025. Keep a small cash reserve, a charged power bank and offline maps.

SAFETY APPS

AlertCops: the official free app of the Policía Nacional and Guardia Civil. It lets you report an incident or call for help discreetly, with a panic button that sends your location to the police. It has a live chat translation function, so you can describe what happened in your own language.

Offline maps and shared location: download offline maps of every region you visit, and share your live location with a friend or family member whenever you're out alone at night or hiking.

Your embassy's alerts: subscribe to your government's travel-alert service if it has one, so you hear about strikes, protests, wildfires and other disruption before it reaches you.

Transport

Spain is large, mountainous and unevenly connected: the high-speed rail network radiates from Madrid and runs superbly along the main corridors, the coach network fills most of the gaps, and a few corners need a car or a lot of patience. The practical rule is simple. Take the train between the big cities, the bus to everywhere else, a flight for the islands, and hire a car only for the places that genuinely need one. Book ahead when it saves money — and in Spain, it often does.

TRAINS

Who runs what: Renfe is the state railway, with the AVE high-speed trains, its low-cost high-speed brand Avlo, and the slower long-distance and regional services (Alvia, Avant, Media Distancia). On the busiest high-speed routes it now competes with two others: Ouigo, the French-owned low-cost operator, and Iryo, a private full-service operator. Between Madrid and Barcelona, Madrid and Valencia, and Madrid and Andalusia you can compare all three, and competition keeps fares surprisingly low.

Fares behave like airline fares: the cheapest seats go first and prices rise as the date approaches, so book as early as you can. Renfe tickets are in theory on sale around 60 days ahead, with AVE tickets usually going on sale a little earlier, at around 90 days, though booking horizons vary; Ouigo and Iryo often release tickets further ahead. Ouigo sells online only and its cheapest fares start at around €9. Trainline sells tickets for all three operators; Renfe's own website can be awkward from outside Spain, which is a good reason to use Trainline or the operators' apps.

Baggage rules differ. Ouigo and Avlo check bags at the boarding gate, and Iryo's cheapest "Inicial" fare is carry-on only. Read the luggage terms before you pay, particularly with a full rucksack.

Allow time at the station. AVE stations have airport-style security with bag scanning, and you must be through it before boarding. Arrive early for your first one.

Commuter trains: Cercanías (around the larger cities) and Rodalies (in Catalonia) are the commuter networks. They need no seat reservation and are the cheap way to reach airports, beaches and towns near cities.

Rail passes: Interrail and Eurail work in Spain, but almost all long-distance trains require a paid reservation on top of your pass — roughly €6 to €15 a journey — and the number of seats open to pass-holders can sell out. Ouigo, Avlo and many Iryo fares don't accept passes at all. If most of your travelling will be in Spain, point-to-point tickets are usually better value than a pass. Renfe also sells its own Spain Rail Pass, valid on its long-distance trains.

INTERCITY COACHES

ALSA is Spain's biggest coach company and goes almost everywhere, including many places the train doesn't. FlixBus runs on the main intercity and international corridors, and regional companies serve the rest. Coaches are usually cheaper than trains on routes where the rail option is slow, and they are often the only practical way to reach smaller towns, national parks and the interior. Tickets can be bought online or at the estación de autobuses; for the cheapest fares and a guaranteed seat on weekends and holidays, book online.

BlaBlaCar (ride-sharing) is popular in Spain and is often cheaper than the coach and quicker. Agree the pick-up point in writing and check the driver's profile and reviews.

CITY TRANSPORT

Metros operate in several cities including Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia, Seville and Bilbao, supplemented by trams, buses and the Cercanías/Rodalies commuter lines. City buses typically run from around 06:00 to 23:00, with metros later and night buses running overnight. Multi-trip cards or passes are usually cheaper than single tickets, so check the local operator's website for the best option for your stay.

Pickpockets work the metros (see the Safety section of the pre-flight advice). Keep your bag in front of you and your phone out of sight near the doors.

From the airport: most major airports have a metro, train or cheap airport-bus link to the centre. Use those first before considering a taxi, and ignore touts offering rides in the arrivals hall.

TAXIS AND RIDE-HAILING

Taxis: use licensed taxis from a rank or booked by app or phone. In most cities a green light or a Libre sign shows that the taxi is free. Using an unlicensed taxi is risky, and passengers caught doing so can be fined up to €600. Licensing rules differ between cities and some require pre-booking.

Apps: FREE NOW and Cabify are widely used in Spain, and Uber and Bolt operate in some cities. Availability and rules differ by city, so check what works where you are. Always check that the car and driver match the app before you get in.

DOMESTIC FLIGHTS AND THE ISLANDS

Flights: Iberia, Vueling, Ryanair, Air Europa and Volotea fly between mainland cities, and flights are the only practical way to reach the Canary Islands from the mainland if you don't have days to spare. On the Madrid–Barcelona corridor and other high-speed routes, a train is usually as fast as flying from city centre to city centre, with no airport transfer, so don't automatically assume the plane is quicker. Budget airlines charge for cabin and hold baggage, so work out the real fare.

Ferries: Baleària and other operators run ferries from the mainland to the Balearic Islands. In the Canary Islands, inter-island flights (Binter) and ferries (including Baleària and Naviera Armas) link the islands. Book ahead for cars, cabins and summer weekends.

HIRE CARS AND DRIVING

Spain drives on the right. If you are used to driving on the left — in the UK, Ireland, South Africa, Australia, New Zealand or India — take the first day slowly. Roundabouts and junctions are where visitors make mistakes: give way to traffic already on the roundabout, and take extra care pulling out after a break.

What you need: a valid licence and ID, and usually a credit card for the deposit. Minimum ages and licence-held periods vary by company, and young drivers are often charged a surcharge. UK photocard licences are valid in Spain; if your licence was issued elsewhere, ask the hire company or the Spanish consulate whether you also need an International Driving Permit. Read the excess and insurance terms carefully, photograph the car before you drive away, and get the fuel type right: gasolina is petrol, gasóleo or diésel is diesel.

Speed limits: 120 km/h on motorways and dual carriageways, 90 km/h on conventional rural roads, 50 km/h in urban areas — and 30 km/h on single-carriageway urban streets with one lane in each direction, which includes most small-town and city-centre streets. Signs override these. Spain has plenty of speed cameras.

Drink-driving: the limit is 0.5 g/l blood (0.25 mg/l breath), and 0.3 g/l (0.15 mg/l breath) for drivers who have held their licence for less than two years and for professional drivers. Police run roadside breath and drug tests. If you've been drinking, don't drive.

Fines: if you can't show that you are resident in Spain, an officer can require you to pay or deposit the fine on the spot before you can continue. Paying promptly gets a 50% reduction, so pay up if you're guilty. A mobile phone in your hand is illegal even if you've pulled over, and earpieces are banned too.

Tolls: most main roads, including the autovías (free dual carriageways), are toll-free. Motorways marked AP (autopistas) charge tolls; keep a card or cash handy.

Breakdowns and accidents: since January 2026, Spanish-registered cars, including hire cars, must carry a connected V16 emergency beacon in place of warning triangles; the hire company has to provide it, so ask where it is. Foreign-registered cars may still use triangles. The beacon does not call the emergency services, so call 112 or 062 once it is on.

Parking: in many cities, blue-marked bays are pay-and-display and green-marked bays are mainly for residents; read the machine and the signs. Cities also run low-emission zones (see the pre-flight advice). Never leave anything visible in a parked car, and don't leave luggage in it overnight.

Cyclists: you must leave a gap of at least 1.5 metres when overtaking a cyclist.

MOTORBIKES AND SCOOTERS

You must be 16 to ride a motorcycle up to 125cc and 18 for anything larger, and you must carry a valid licence for the vehicle — check your insurance will cover you before you hire. Wear a helmet. Scooter hire is easy to find on the coasts and islands, and it's also where many travellers come to grief: the roads are unfamiliar, the surfaces can be slippery, and alcohol and a rented scooter are a bad mix.

HITCHHIKING

It is not common in Spain, and you can't hitch on motorways. Use BlaBlaCar or the coach instead.

Food & Drink

Eating in Spain is one of the best reasons to be there, and — handled properly — one of the cheapest. The catch is timing: the country runs on a different clock from most of Northern Europe, kitchens close in the afternoon, and arriving at a restaurant at 6 p.m. will get you a coffee and a puzzled look. Learn the rhythm, eat your big meal at lunchtime, and let the bars do the rest.

Read More

THE SPANISH DAY AT THE TABLE

Breakfast (desayuno) is light: coffee and a pastry, or tostada con tomate y aceite (toast with grated tomato and olive oil), at a café between about 07:00 and 12:00.

The pre-lunch drink (el aperitivo): between roughly 12:00 and 14:00 it is very typical to meet friends for a caña and some tapas. This is the best time to graze in a bar.

Lunch (la comida) is the main meal of the day, served from about 13:00 to 16:00 and most commonly eaten around 14:00. Restaurants are packed, and this is when the menú del día is on offer.

Dinner (la cena) is lighter and later. Restaurants typically serve from about 20:00 to 23:30; many Spaniards don't sit down until 21:00 or later, and in Madrid many restaurants don't open for dinner much before 21:00. Between lunch and dinner most restaurant kitchens are closed, so if you miss lunch, you'll be looking for a café, a tapas bar or a supermarket. Plan accordingly, particularly on Sundays and in small towns.

THE MENÚ DEL DÍA

The single best budget tool in Spain. On weekdays at lunchtime most restaurants offer a fixed-price menu of a starter, a main, a dessert or coffee, bread and a drink (often wine or beer included), for far less than the same dishes ordered separately. Expect roughly €11 to €20 depending on where you are, with the lower prices in provincial towns and the upper end in central Madrid, Barcelona and the Basque Country. Look for the blackboard outside or the handwritten sheet on the bar. Many places serve it only on weekdays, and not at weekends.

The best-value places are the ones full of workers in high-vis jackets, with a handwritten menu and no photographs. The worst are the ones with a laminated photo menu in six languages and a person outside beckoning you in.

TAPAS, PINTXOS AND RACIONES

Tapa: a small portion of food served with a drink. In Granada and parts of Andalusia, a free tapa with each drink is still the custom, which makes bar-hopping a cheap dinner. Elsewhere you order and pay for them.

Pintxo (or pincho): the Basque Country's version: small items served on bread, often held together with a cocktail stick, laid out on the bar counter. You help yourself or order from the blackboard, and in many bars the bill is calculated from what you've eaten and from the sticks on your plate. Ask how the bar charges if you're not sure.

Ración and media ración: a full plate, or half a plate, meant for sharing. A few of these between a group of friends make a meal. Ask how big they are before you order.

Tapeo: the custom of moving from bar to bar, with one drink and one or two plates in each, rather than sitting in one place all evening. It is the most sociable way to eat and one of the best ways to find out what a town is really like.

WHAT TO EAT, REGION BY REGION

Everywhere: tortilla española (thick potato omelette), jamón (cured ham — ibérico is the premium kind), croquetas, patatas bravas, pimientos de Padrón (small green peppers, mostly mild, a few hot), and bocadillos (filled baguettes — the cheapest meal in Spain).

Andalusia: gazpacho and salmorejo (cold tomato soups — a lifesaver in a heatwave), pescaíto frito (fried fish), jamón from the mountain villages, and sherry in Jerez.

Madrid and Castile: cocido madrileño (a hearty chickpea stew), bocadillo de calamares (fried-squid sandwich), roast suckling pig in Segovia.

Extremadura: top-quality ibérico ham, migas (fried breadcrumbs with chorizo or pork), and cheeses such as torta del Casar.

Catalonia: pa amb tomàquet (bread rubbed with tomato), escalivada (roasted vegetables), crema catalana (a custard dessert like crème brûlée), and calçots (grilled spring onions eaten with a sauce, in season in late winter and spring).

Valencia: the home of paella. The original is a rice dish cooked with chicken, rabbit and beans, not with seafood and not the garish yellow plates on the seafront. Eat it at lunchtime, away from the tourist strips, and see whether it is made to order (it takes time, and it should be). Beware of places that offer it at any hour.

The Basque Country: pintxos in San Sebastián and Bilbao, bacalao (salt cod), grilled fish and steak, and txakoli (a lightly sparkling white wine poured from a height).

Galicia: pulpo a la gallega (octopus with paprika and olive oil), empanada gallega (a savoury pie), and seafood so fresh it barely needs cooking.

Asturias: fabada (a bean and pork stew), strong blue cheeses, and sidra (cider), poured from a height into a glass.

The Canary Islands: papas arrugadas con mojo (small wrinkled potatoes with a red or green sauce), and fresh fish.

The Balearic Islands: ensaimada (a spiral pastry) and sobrasada (a soft, spicy cured sausage).

Sweet things: churros con chocolate (fried dough sticks with thick hot chocolate for dipping — a breakfast or late-night treat), flan, turrón (nougat, especially around Christmas) and pestiños and other fried pastries around festivals.

VEGETARIANS, VEGANS AND ALLERGIES

Vegetarians: Spain is better than it was but still meat-heavy. Beware of "vegetarian" dishes that contain ham, bacon bits (taquitos de jamón), anchovies or tuna, and of stocks made from meat or fish. Say "Soy vegetariano/a. No como carne ni pescado. ¿Lleva jamón o caldo de carne?" (I'm vegetarian. I don't eat meat or fish. Does it contain ham or meat stock?). Reliable options include tortilla (check for chorizo), pimientos de Padrón, patatas bravas, gazpacho (usually vegan), ensalada mixta (which often comes with tuna — ask for it sin atún), grilled vegetables and pan con tomate. Cities such as Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia have a good selection of vegetarian and vegan restaurants.

Allergies: restaurants must be able to tell you whether a dish contains any of the 14 allergens listed in EU rules (including gluten, nuts, shellfish, eggs, milk, fish and sulphites). Say "Soy alérgico/a a…" (I'm allergic to…) and the allergen, and ask if you're in doubt: "¿Lleva…?" (Does it contain…?). For coeliac disease, sin gluten is the phrase. Write the allergen down on a card or in a translation app, particularly in rural areas where English is less common.

SUPERMARKETS AND SELF-CATERING

Mercadona, Lidl, Carrefour and Dia are the cheap, reliable chains, and most towns have a covered market (mercado) with excellent fruit, vegetables, cheese, ham and fish at decent prices. Shops across Spain commonly close on Sundays and smaller ones may shut for a midday break, so buy what you need on Saturday. Most hostels have kitchens; the cheapest day of all is a supermarket breakfast, a menú del día for lunch and a bar snack for dinner.

COFFEE

Café solo — a short black espresso. Cortado — an espresso with a little milk. Café con leche — about half coffee, half hot milk, the standard morning drink. Café con hielo — a hot espresso served with a glass of ice, which you pour over yourself. Americano — a long black. Carajillo — coffee with a measure of brandy or other spirit. Bombón — espresso with condensed milk (a Valencian speciality). Ask for descafeinado if you want decaf. A coffee at the bar is usually cheaper than at a table, and cheaper still than on a terrace in a tourist area.

WHAT TO DRINK

Beer: a caña is a small draught beer, the standard order in most of Spain (in Madrid and some other cities, a tubo is a taller glass); a jarra is a large glass; a botellín is a small bottle. Regional beers include Estrella Galicia (Galicia), Estrella Damm (Barcelona), Mahou (Madrid) and Cruzcampo (Seville). A clara is beer with lemonade — a long, light drink for the heat.

Wine: vino tinto (red), blanco (white), rosado (rosé). The big names are Rioja and Ribera del Duero for reds, and Albariño from Galicia and Verdejo from Rueda for whites. A glass (copa) in a bar is often inexpensive. Tinto de verano — red wine with fizzy lemonade, over ice — is the local summer drink, lighter and cheaper than sangría, which is mostly made for tourists. Calimocho is red wine mixed with cola, a street-drinking staple.

Other drinks: vermut (vermouth), served on ice with an olive and a slice of orange as an aperitif; sidra (Asturian cider); cava (Spanish sparkling wine, mostly from Catalonia); sherry (jerez) in Andalusia — dry fino and manzanilla go well with tapas; and horchata, a sweet, chilled tiger-nut drink, in Valencia. Spirits and mixers are copas or cubatas and are poured generously.

Water: ask for agua del grifo (tap water) if you want it free; tap water is safe to drink in most of mainland Spain. Agua con gas is sparkling.

The rules: you have to be 18 to drink in Spain, and drinking in the street is banned in many places, with fines. See the Drinking & Nightlife Laws in the pre-flight advice.

EATING OUT: HOW IT WORKS

Getting attention: at a bar, catch the barman's eye, say "Perdona" or "Oye" (hey), and order. You don't wait to be seated at a bar, and the waiter will not rush over to you. At a restaurant, wait to be shown to a table if there is a host.

The bill: you must ask for it — "La cuenta, por favor" — because bringing the bill unasked would be rude. Many bars total your bill at the end rather than charging for each round.

Cover charges and terraces: some restaurants add a small per-person charge for bread or a table setting (cubierto), and terraces often cost more than the bar or an inside table. Prices are on the menu; check before you sit down.

Tipping: not obligatory. Round up or leave small change for good service. See the pre-flight advice on money.

Sobremesa: the unhurried conversation at the table after a meal. Don't expect to be moved on, and don't rush it yourself.

Rounds: in a group it is common to buy rounds, with each person paying for a round in turn, rather than splitting the bill item by item. In some traditional bars, napkins and shells end up on the floor — if the floor is covered in them, that's the custom.

Language

Spanish is one of the easiest languages for an English speaker to pronounce and one of the most useful to speak — and in Spain, a few words go a long way. English is common in the main tourist areas and among younger people, and much less so in rural areas, in family-run bars and with older people. Learn the basic phrases, always start with a greeting, and you'll be treated noticeably better. Spain also has several regional languages, which you'll meet on road signs, menus and station announcements from the first day.

THE LANGUAGES OF SPAIN

Spanish (español or castellano) is the official language of the whole country, and everyone understands it.

Four other languages are co-official in their regions, and you will see them everywhere:

Catalan (català) in Catalonia and the Balearic Islands, and Valencian (valencià), a closely related variety, in the Valencian region. Signs in Barcelona will usually be in Catalan first.

Galician (galego) in Galicia, which is close to Portuguese.

Basque (euskara) in the Basque Country and parts of Navarre. It is unrelated to Spanish or any other language, and looks very unlike anything else you'll see on a sign — lots of x, tz and k.

Aranese (a form of Occitan) in the Val d'Aran in the Pyrenees.

You do not need to speak a regional language to visit these regions: Spanish works everywhere. But a "thank you" or "good morning" in the local language is a gesture that goes down well, and it helps to know that a station may be listed under a Catalan or Basque name. Don't call a regional language a dialect — it is a language, and people care.

PRONUNCIATION IN A MINUTE

Spanish is spelt almost exactly as it sounds, and vowels are always the same: a as in "father", e as in "bed", i as in "see", o as in "off", u as in "boot".

h is silent (hola sounds like "OH-la"). j (and g before e or i) is a throaty "h" sound, as in jamón. ll sounds like "y" in most places. ñ is "ny", as in España. qu is a "k", so queso is "KEH-so". rr and a word-initial r are rolled.

c before e or i, and z, sound like the "th" in "think" in most of Spain (gracias is "GRA-thyas"), but like an "s" in Andalusia, the Canary Islands and Latin America. Both are right; follow the people around you. b and v sound almost the same.

Stress falls on the second-to-last syllable of words ending in a vowel, n or s, and on the last syllable of other words; an accent mark overrides this. Questions are framed by ¿ … ? and exclamations by ¡ … !.

ESSENTIAL PHRASES

Greetings: Hola (Hello) · Buenos días (Good morning) · Buenas tardes (Good afternoon/evening, used until it gets dark) · Buenas noches (Good night) · ¿Qué tal? (How are you?) · Mucho gusto (Pleased to meet you) · Adiós / Hasta luego (Goodbye / See you later) · Hasta mañana (See you tomorrow).

Politeness: Por favor (Please) · Gracias / Muchas gracias (Thanks / Thank you very much) · De nada (You're welcome) · Perdón / Perdona / Disculpe (Excuse me / sorry) · Lo siento (I'm sorry) · Sí / No (Yes / No) · Vale (OK).

Getting by: No hablo español (I don't speak Spanish) · No entiendo (I don't understand) · ¿Habla inglés? (Do you speak English?) · ¿Puede hablar más despacio, por favor? (Can you speak more slowly, please?) · ¿Cómo se dice … en español? (How do you say … in Spanish?) · ¿Qué significa? (What does it mean?) · No sé (I don't know).

Shopping and eating: ¿Cuánto cuesta? / ¿Cuánto es? (How much is it?) · Quisiera … (I would like …) · Me pone … (Give me …, the standard bar order) · Una caña, por favor (A small beer, please) · La cuenta, por favor (The bill, please) · ¿Se puede pagar con tarjeta? (Can I pay by card?) · Sin … (Without …) · Para llevar (To take away).

Directions: ¿Dónde está …? (Where is …?) · ¿Cómo llego a …? (How do I get to …?) · a la derecha (right) · a la izquierda (left) · todo recto (straight ahead) · cerca / lejos (near / far) · los servicios / el baño (the toilet) · la estación (the station) · la parada (the stop).

NUMBERS AND DAYS

1–10: uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez. Bigger: veinte (20), cincuenta (50), cien (100), mil (1,000).

Days: lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes, sábado, domingo (Monday to Sunday). Time words: hoy (today), mañana (tomorrow, and also "morning"), ayer (yesterday), ahora (now).

SPAIN-SPECIFIC SPANISH

The Spanish you learn from apps and Latin American television is different in places from the Spanish spoken in Spain:

Vosotros: Spain uses vosotros for the informal "you" plural, where Latin America uses ustedes. Tú versus usted: tú (informal "you") is used very widely in Spain, including between strangers of a similar age, in bars and in hostels. Use usted with older people, officials and anyone you want to show formal respect to.

Vocabulary: coche (car), móvil (mobile phone), ordenador (computer), zumo (juice), patata (potato), billete (ticket).

Everyday slang: vale (OK, fine — used constantly), guay (cool), mola (it's cool), tío / tía (mate, literally "uncle/aunt"), colega (mate), chulo/a (nice, cool — though it can also mean cocky, depending on tone), flipar (to be amazed), currar (to work), pasta (money), cutre (shabby), and guiri (a foreign tourist — usually said without malice, but it isn't a compliment). Botellón is the street-drinking party you shouldn't join (see the drinking laws).

REGIONAL PHRASES

Catalan: Bon dia (good morning) · Bona tarda (good afternoon) · Gràcies (thanks) · Si us plau (please) · Perdó (sorry/excuse me) · Adéu (goodbye).

Galician: Bos días (good morning) · Grazas (thanks) · Por favor (please) · Adeus (goodbye).

Basque: Kaixo (hello) · Egun on (good morning) · Eskerrik asko (thank you very much) · Mesedez (please) · Agur (goodbye).

HOW TO PICK IT UP FAST

Start every interaction — shop, bar, taxi, hostel desk — with hola or buenos días; it is the simplest courtesy and it changes the response. Practise on a short list of phrases you need every day (ordering, asking, paying, thanking). Download Spanish for offline use in a translation app (see the pre-flight apps section) and use the camera mode for menus and signs. If you want a more serious start, a week or two at a language school will transform the trip — see the language schools advice. In hostels, a language exchange with other travellers or staff is the cheapest practice there is, and Spaniards are generally patient with attempts at their language.

Spanish Etiquette

Spaniards are warm, direct, sociable and a little noisy, and they're quick to forgive visitors who make an effort. Most of the etiquette that matters here is about rhythm and respect: greeting people, fitting in with the daily timetable, and not behaving as if the country were a theme park. Spain is also receiving more visitors than some of its residents are comfortable with, so good manners are noticed.

Read More

GREETINGS

Say hello first. Greet people when you enter a small shop, a bar or a lift, and say goodbye when you leave (Hola or Buenos días, then Adiós or Gracias, hasta luego). Starting a request without a greeting is the single commonest way for visitors to seem rude.

Handshakes and kisses: in formal and business settings, a handshake. Among friends and in social settings, dos besos — two kisses, one on each cheek — is the usual greeting, between women and between a woman and a man, and for men who know one another well it may be a hug or a handshake. If you're introduced to someone at a hostel or a party, expect it. If you'd rather not, offer your hand first and no one will mind.

Names and titles: first names are normal almost immediately. Use usted and señor / señora with older people and officials until they invite you to relax.

TIME AND THE DAILY RHYTHM

The day is shifted late — breakfast is light, lunch is mid-afternoon, dinner is after nine, and bars stay busy into the small hours (see Food & Drink). Don't schedule dinner for 18:00 and don't expect a kitchen to be open outside meal times.

Opening hours: shops in the bigger towns are often open continuously from about 10:00 to 21:00, but smaller shops and many offices close for a midday break (roughly 14:00 to 17:00, particularly in summer). Sunday is the normal closing day for shops and many businesses. Town halls and government offices generally open in the mornings and have shorter hours in summer, so don't count on doing paperwork in the afternoon.

Public holidays: national holidays include 1 January, 6 January, Good Friday, 1 May, 15 August, 12 October, 1 November, 8 December and 25 December, and every region and town adds its own. Expect shops, banks and some transport to be closed or reduced, and book ahead for travel the days around them.

August: many Spaniards take their holiday in August, and small businesses in some towns close for part of it. The coast gets crowded and expensive; some city restaurants close; and it is very hot.

Punctuality: social punctuality is relaxed — arriving a little late to meet friends is normal. Trains, flights, tours and tickets with timed entry are not, so be on time for anything with a timetable.

NOISE, SPACE AND CONVERSATION

Spaniards talk loudly, interrupt each other, stand close and touch your arm while they speak. It is warmth rather than aggression. Heated, animated arguments about football or politics often end with everyone buying each other a drink.

At the same time, residential neighbourhoods are noisy at night and quiet in the siesta hours; be considerate in hostels, apartments and old town streets, particularly when you return late. Dragging a wheeled suitcase over cobbles at 3 a.m. is how tourists make enemies.

BARS, RESTAURANTS AND PAYING

See Food & Drink for the detail. The essentials: catch the barman's eye and say "Perdona" or "Oye" to order; you'll have to ask for the bill; rounds are the norm in groups; tipping is small and optional; and drinking is slow and sociable — Spaniards take hours to drink a few cañas, and being visibly drunk in public is frowned on.

DRESS

Churches and cathedrals: many expect covered shoulders and knees, and some turn away visitors who don't comply. Carry a light scarf or shirt. Remove hats inside, switch your phone to silent, and don't walk around during a service.

Towns versus beaches: beachwear belongs on the beach. In some places it is illegal to walk around in just a bikini or swim shorts, or bare-chested, and you can be fined on seafront promenades and the streets behind them. Put a T-shirt or wrap on between the sand and the bar.

Topless sunbathing is common on many beaches, and naturist beaches exist; follow what the people around you are doing and the signs.

FIESTAS, PROCESSIONS AND RELIGION

Religious festivals — particularly Holy Week — are serious events for many people, even in a country that is increasingly secular. Don't walk through a procession, don't use flash in a procession or inside a church, and keep voices down. For secular fiestas, join in: it's the point. See the fiestas and festivals advice.

REGIONAL IDENTITY AND TOUCHY SUBJECTS

Spain is a country of strong regional identities, and in Catalonia, the Basque Country and Galicia many people feel their region's language and culture is as important as the national one. A few guidelines:

— Don't call Catalan, Galician or Basque "dialects", and don't insist on Spanish when someone has greeted you in another language; just reply in Spanish or English politely.
— Politics is passionate, particularly Catalan independence and the monarchy. Listen more than you opine.
— The Civil War and the Franco dictatorship are still raw for many families. Treat the subject with care and don't make jokes about it.
— Bullfighting is legal in much of Spain but divisive, and many Spaniards dislike it as much as many visitors do. You don't need to attend or defend it; it is a conversation you may be drawn into.

BEING A GOOD GUEST IN A BUSY COUNTRY

In recent years there have been protests against mass tourism in several Spanish cities and islands, mostly about housing costs and crowding. Behaviour that is invisible at home — shouting in the street at night, wandering in swimwear, treating a residential block as an attraction — is exactly what residents complain about. You can be part of the solution: spend money in local businesses, stay in licensed accommodation, keep noise down after dark, don't drink in the street, and treat local people as neighbours rather than staff.

PHOTOGRAPHY, PHONES AND SMOKING

Photography: ask before taking close photographs of people, especially children and anyone in a procession. Some churches, museums and palaces ban photography or flash; look for signs. Drones have their own regulations and need specific permission in many places, so check before flying one.

Smoking: it is banned in enclosed public places, and a growing number of places restrict it on beaches and in some outdoor areas. Look for signs, and never stub a cigarette out in the countryside in summer — causing a wildfire, even unintentionally, is a criminal offence.

LGBTQ+ TRAVELLERS AND OTHER IDENTITIES

Spain is generally tolerant, same-sex marriage has been legal for years, and big cities and many resorts have LGBTQ+ venues and large Pride celebrations. Standard city precautions apply. Discrimination of any kind is not unheard of; if you have a problem, report it to the police and your embassy.

HOSTEL ETIQUETTE

Spanish hostels are social, and a late night is considered normal. That makes courtesy to dorm-mates more important, not less. Keep noise down when you come in, use your phone torch rather than the main light, pack your bag the night before, and don't use a shared dorm as a place to continue a party. Use your padlock; don't leave valuables around; and clean up after cooking.

Fun & Games

Every hostel common room has a pack of cards, a chess set with a missing pawn and a table football table that someone is taking far too seriously. Spain adds something better: a whole tradition of games played over a drink in a bar, usually by four locals who have played together for decades, with a deck of cards that looks like it escaped from a medieval tarot reading and rules nobody will write down. Learn a couple and you will be welcomed at tables you'd otherwise only watch. This section covers the Spanish deck, three games that work in a hostel, the bar-room classics, and some games that need nothing but a bit of Spanish.

THE SPANISH DECK (LA BARAJA ESPAÑOLA)

Spain's cards aren't the 52-card pack you know. The baraja española has 40 cards in four suits:

Oros (coins, gold) · Copas (cups) · Espadas (swords) · Bastos (clubs or batons).

Each suit has ten cards: As (ace, "1"), 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and three face cards — Sota (the page or jack, "10"), Caballo (the horse and rider, "11") and Rey (the king, "12"). There are no 8s, 9s or 10s.

No Spanish deck? Make one. Take a standard pack and remove the 8s, 9s and 10s. Use the jack for the Sota, the queen for the Caballo and the king for the Rey. The usual match for suits is diamonds = Oros, hearts = Copas, spades = Espadas and clubs = Bastos. A real Spanish deck is inexpensive and well worth buying as a souvenir; Fournier, from Vitoria in the Basque Country, is the best-known brand.

Spanish card games vary from region to region and from bar to bar. If locals tell you "that's not how we play it here", they're right, and so are the people in the next town. Always agree the rules and the target score before you deal.

ESCOBA (THE "BROOM")

Players: 2 to 4 (four can play as two partnerships). Deck: Spanish, 40 cards. Idea: capture cards from the table by matching them to your card so that the total is 15, and sweep the table clear for a bonus.

Card values: the Ace is 1, the 2 to 7 are worth their numbers, and the face cards count as Sota = 8, Caballo = 9, Rey = 10 (not the numbers printed on them). That is why a standard deck without the 8, 9 and 10 works so well here: jack = 8, queen = 9, king = 10.

Setup: deal three cards to each player, and put four face-up in the middle of the table.

Play: going round, each player plays one card from their hand. If that card plus one or more cards on the table adds up to 15, you capture all of those cards and put them in a pile in front of you. You may choose between possible combinations. If you can't make 15, your card goes onto the table to join the others. When everyone has played all three cards, deal another three each from the deck (without adding more to the table) and carry on until the deck runs out. In the standard rules, any cards left on the table after the last play go to the player who made the last capture.

The escoba: if your capture clears the entire table, you've made an escoba (a sweep) and score a point; leave one of the captured cards face up in your pile to mark it.

Scoring: after the last card, count your points. Typical points are one each for: the most cards captured; the most oros (the coin suit); the 7 of oros (the siete de velo — the "lucky seven"); the most sevens (some groups use the traditional "prime" count instead); and one for each escoba. Scoring details differ from group to group, so settle them before you start. Play to an agreed total — 15 or 31 are common.

Tip: keep an eye on the sevens and the oros, and don't leave the table in a state that makes it easy for your opponent to sweep it.

BRISCA

Players: 2, or 4 as two teams of two (partners sit opposite). Deck: Spanish, 40 cards. Idea: win tricks that contain valuable cards; the team with more than 60 of the 120 points available wins.

Setup: deal three cards to each player. Turn the next card face up beside the deck: its suit is trumps (the brisca) for the whole game. Leave the remaining deck face down, with the turned-up card tucked underneath.

Play: the first player leads any card. Everyone else plays one card in turn. You don't have to follow suit — you can play any card from your hand. The trick is won by the highest trump played; if no trump was played, by the highest card of the suit that was led. The winner takes the cards and then draws the top card of the deck first, followed by the others in turn, so everyone is back to three cards. The winner leads the next trick. The last player to draw takes the face-up trump card. When the deck is gone, play out the last three tricks.

Card order and points: from highest to lowest, the order is As (Ace) – 3 – Rey – Caballo – Sota – 7 – 6 – 5 – 4 – 2. The point values:

As = 11 · Tres (3) = 10 · Rey = 4 · Caballo = 3 · Sota = 2 · all the others (7, 6, 5, 4, 2) = 0.

The total in the pack is 120. After the last trick, each side adds up the values of the cards they've won. More than 60 wins; exactly 60 each is a draw.

Tip: the Ace and the Three of trumps are the game's big cards. Lead low cards to pass the lead without giving anything away, play a trump when your opponent has led a card worth points, and keep count of which trumps have gone. Some people play a house rule that lets you swap the 7 of trumps for the face-up trump card — agree beforehand.

MUS

Mus is the real thing: one of the most widely played card games in Spain, with its roots in the Basque Country, and a fixture of bars, social clubs (peñas) and village fiestas. It is played by four people in two fixed partnerships, with partners sitting opposite one another, using the Spanish deck. Each hand is a series of betting rounds on different aspects of your cards — high cards (grande), low cards (chica), pairs (pares) and a total of 31 or more (juego) — and the winners are the first side to reach a target score, commonly 30 or 40. Before a round starts you decide whether to discard and draw ("mus"), or to go straight to the betting. Partners may exchange secret signs (señas) agreed beforehand — and bluffing is a big part of it.

How to learn: don't try to learn it from a description. Find a hostel with a Basque or northern-Spanish member of staff, or ask at a bar where people are playing, and watch a few hands. The rules vary by region, and the locals will be proud to explain them.

DOMINOES (DOMINÓ)

Dominoes is a fixture in Spanish bars and village squares, and the sound of tiles being slammed on a table is part of the soundtrack of an afternoon.

Equipment: a standard "double-six" set of 28 tiles. Players: 2 to 4. Setup: shuffle the tiles face down and take seven each. In a four-player game it is usually played as two teams of partners sitting opposite one another.

Play: the player with the highest double starts by placing it in the middle of the table. In turn, players place a tile from their hand with a matching number at one end of the line, joining it to the open end of a tile already down; a double is usually placed crossways. If you can't play, you pass (in the version with a draw pile, you draw until you can). The first player to play all of their tiles wins the hand. If everyone is blocked, the player with the lowest total number of dots in their hand wins. Agree the scoring and the target before you start.

PETANCA

Petanca is the Spanish form of boules, played in parks and squares across Spain; look for the sandy courts (pistas) in town parks.

Equipment: a small target ball (the boliche) and a set of heavy metal balls (bolas). Players: teams of one to three.

Play: a player throws the small ball a few metres. A player from the first team throws a metal ball to land as near to the small ball as possible; then the other team throws, and from then on the team whose ball is not closest keeps throwing until it takes the lead. When all the balls are thrown, the team whose ball is closest to the small ball scores one point for every ball that is closer than the opponents' best ball. Play to 13 points. You can knock the other team's balls away, which is what makes it fun.

FUTBOLÍN (TABLE FOOTBALL)

The table football (foosball) table is the unofficial centrepiece of many Spanish bars and hostels. Standard etiquette: agree the rules first. A common house rule is "no spinning" — you must not whirl the rods round fully — and many players insist on it. Winner stays on; losers buy the next round.

GAMES THAT NEED ONLY SPANISH

These cost nothing, work on a long bus ride, and double as language practice.

Veo, veo (I spy): the Spanish version is a call-and-response: "Veo, veo." — "¿Qué ves?" — "Una cosita." — "¿Y qué cosita es?" — "Empieza por la letra …" (It starts with the letter …). The others guess. Colours, numbers and the names of everyday objects come up all the time.

Palabras encadenadas (word chain): one player says a Spanish word; the next must say a word that begins with the last syllable of the previous word, and so on, until someone is stuck or repeats a word. Set a time limit of a few seconds.

Piedra, papel o tijera (rock, paper, scissors) and pares o nones (evens or odds): both are used to decide who goes first or who pays. In pares o nones, two people secretly choose to hold out some fingers, and one calls "pares" (even) or "nones" (odd) before they count the total together.

Mímica (charades): act out a film, a person or a phrase without speaking. It works brilliantly in a group with several languages.

The Spanish-only rule: an evening in which all the games must be played in Spanish, with a small forfeit (a round of drinks, a song) for speaking English. It sounds silly and it works.

ON THE BEACH

Palas (beach bats): the sound of Spanish beaches is the ping of a rubber ball hitting a wooden paddle. Two players hit the ball back and forth with solid wooden bats, trying to keep the rally going as long as possible. There is no score and no net, and it's harder than it looks.

COMMON-ROOM ETIQUETTE

Ask before you join in and be welcome; don't hog the pool or table-football table; agree the rules first; tidy up the cards; and don't treat a Spanish game as a joke — people take them very seriously. If a game is going on at a bar, don't interrupt, and don't comment on someone's play unless you're asked. If you win, be gracious; if you lose, buy the round.

Onward Travel

Spain is a natural hinge between continents: Portugal is next door, France is a short train ride from Barcelona, Morocco is a ferry ride from Andalusia, and tiny Andorra and Gibraltar are within easy reach. Moving between Spain and its Schengen neighbours is effortless, but the exceptions — Morocco, Andorra and Gibraltar — each have their own paperwork. This section covers the ways out of Spain, what to expect at the other end, and how it affects your 90 days.

Read More

YOUR SCHENGEN CLOCK

Spain, Portugal and France are all in the Schengen area, so crossing between them does not stop or restart your 90-day allowance. Days spent in any Schengen country count towards the same 90 days in any 180. A trip to Morocco, Andorra or the UK takes you outside Schengen, so those days do not count against the allowance, and you re-enter through a border check when you return. If you hold a single-entry Schengen visa, leaving Schengen ends it: you can't re-enter on the same visa, so if you plan a side-trip to Morocco or Andorra, make sure your visa is a multiple-entry one. For the rules on EES and ETIAS, see the pre-flight advice.

PORTUGAL

Border: both countries are in Schengen, so there are no routine passport checks, but carry your passport anyway; you may be asked for ID, and you need it to check into hostels. Portugal is one hour behind Spain.

By rail: only two train routes actually cross the border. The Celta regional train runs between Vigo (Galicia) and Porto, twice a day — but from April 2026 until April 2027 buses are reported to replace trains on part of the line, between Vigo and Valença, so check before you plan around it. The other route, from Badajoz (Extremadura) towards Lisbon, forms part of the Madrid–Lisbon journey, and there is no direct Madrid–Lisbon train: you must change.

By coach: usually the best option. ALSA and FlixBus run services between Spanish cities and Lisbon, Porto and the Algarve, including Seville to Lisbon and Seville to Faro. Madrid to Lisbon takes roughly six to eight hours by coach. In June 2026 FlixBus began new routes between Madrid and Braga (via Zamora and Porto) and between Lisbon and Badajoz (via Évora and Elvas), a useful option if you're in Extremadura.

By air: Madrid–Lisbon is under an hour, with frequent flights.

FRANCE

Border: Schengen on both sides, so no routine checks.

High-speed trains: the TGV runs between Paris and Barcelona, calling at Perpignan, Figueres and Girona, and takes roughly seven hours from Barcelona to Paris. Renfe runs AVE trains from Barcelona to Lyon and Marseille (via Montpellier), and a Madrid–Marseille service, and has planned a Barcelona–Toulouse route (via Girona, Figueres, Perpignan and Carcassonne) — check what is running before you build a plan around it. Seat reservations are compulsory on all of these, including with an Interrail or Eurail pass.

Regional trains: the slow way across the Mediterranean border is by local train through Portbou and Cerbère. In the Basque Country, a short local train (the Euskotren) links Irun in Spain with Hendaye in France.

ANDORRA

What it is: a small, mountainous principality between Spain and France, popular for skiing, hiking and duty-free shopping. Andorra is not in the EU or Schengen and has no airport or railway, so you must travel through Spain or France to reach it.

Border: everyone entering Andorra goes through customs control, and the Schengen entry rules for Spain and France apply to reach it. Duty may be payable on tobacco, alcohol and luxury goods when you return, with strict limits on tobacco, so check the allowances before you shop. There can be long queues on the way back into Spain, particularly on Sunday afternoons and holidays.

Getting there: there are direct buses from Barcelona taking about three hours, and indirect routes combine a train (for example to Puigcerdà in Catalonia) with a bus for the final stretch.

GIBRALTAR

What it is: a British Overseas Territory at the southern tip of Spain, reached on foot from the Spanish town of La Línea de la Concepción. It is famous for the Rock and its monkeys, and is a straightforward day trip from the Costa del Sol.

Border: since the UK–EU agreement on Gibraltar began to apply provisionally on 15 July 2026, there are no longer entry and exit check booths at the land border, and Gibraltar's short-stay entry conditions are aligned with Schengen. Take your passport, and check the current rules before you go, as the arrangements are new and may change.

MOROCCO (AND CEUTA AND MELILLA)

Getting there: ferries run all day between southern Spain and northern Morocco. From Algeciras to Tanger Med takes about one hour 30 minutes and is run by operators including Baleària, FRS and Naviera Armas. Services from Tarifa to Tangier (the city's own port) are operated by Africa Morocco Link and Baleària. Most ferries accept foot passengers and bicycles. The Tanger Med port is some way outside Tangier city, so allow time and money for onward transport. From Barcelona, GNV runs ferries to Tanger Med. Algeciras also has ferries to Ceuta.

Entry: citizens of the UK, EU countries, the USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and many others can enter Morocco without a visa for up to 90 days. You need a passport (national ID cards aren't accepted) valid for at least six months from your date of entry, with spare pages for stamps, and you should expect to show a return or onward ticket. Some nationalities need a visa or e-visa — if you hold a South African passport, or any other not on the visa-free lists, confirm the current rules with the Moroccan embassy before you travel. Keep your entry stamp and any forms until you leave.

Ceuta and Melilla: two Spanish cities on the Moroccan coast, with land borders to Morocco. They are part of Spain, and the border with Morocco is an external frontier of the EU, so you go through an entry check when you cross back from Morocco.

Before you go: read your own government's Morocco travel advice, including the rules on drugs, which are enforced even though cannabis is widely offered to visitors. Many Spanish hire companies don't allow their cars to be taken to Morocco, so check the contract. When you return to Spain you'll be at the Schengen external border again: allow extra time for the checks.

FURTHER AFIELD

Italy and beyond: ferries run from Barcelona to Italian ports including Genoa and Civitavecchia, and there are plenty of budget flights from Spain's airports to the rest of Europe. The UK: as well as flights, Brittany Ferries sails from Santander and Bilbao. Elsewhere: before you book onward travel, check the entry rules of your next country; some require a visa or an electronic travel authorisation, and some require proof of onward travel.

WHEN YOU LEAVE SPAIN

Exit checks: under the EU's EES, your departure from the Schengen area is recorded at the border. Allow extra time at airports, and remember that checks can take place before you board.

Cash: if you carry €10,000 or more in cash when you leave the EU, you must declare it.

Last steps: use up your cash, return your hire car with a full tank, check you have your passport and boarding pass before you leave your accommodation, and don't leave your last euros in the airport shops: change them at home or spend them on a final caña.
