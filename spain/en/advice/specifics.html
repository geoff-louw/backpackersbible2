Fiestas & Festivals

Spain has more festivals than weeks in the year. Every village has a patron saint, every patron saint has a party, and the big ones — the fire festival, the bull run, the tomato fight, the Holy Week processions — draw people from around the world. On top of the traditional fiestas sits one of Europe's strongest summer music circuits and the most famous clubbing island on Earth. This section covers what's worth planning a trip around, when it happens, and how to get through it intact.

THE BIG TRADITIONAL FIESTAS

Semana Santa (Holy Week): the week leading up to Easter Sunday, and the most important religious festival in Spain. Hooded penitents (nazarenos) and elaborate floats (pasos) carrying religious sculptures move through the streets for hours, often by candlelight and to the sound of drums and brass bands. Andalusia stages some of the biggest and most elaborate processions — Seville, Málaga and Granada among them — but towns across the country, from Zamora to Valladolid, hold their own, and each has a different character. It is solemn, not a party. In 2027 the dates are unusually early: Palm Sunday is 21 March and Easter Sunday is 28 March, with Good Friday on 26 March. Book accommodation and transport as early as you can; in Seville, the night from Thursday into Good Friday (the Madrugá) is the best-known.

Las Fallas, Valencia (15–19 March): the city's biggest festival. Neighbourhood groups spend the year building huge satirical sculptures (fallas) out of wood, papier-mâché and polystyrene, which are displayed in the streets for days and then burned in giant bonfires on the final night, 19 March (la Cremà). Expect fireworks and firecrackers at all hours, daytime mascletàs (noise-and-rhythm firework displays) in the main square, parades, paella and very little sleep. Valencia is packed and hostels sell out well ahead.

Feria de Abril, Seville: the fair begins two weeks after Semana Santa, so in April 2027. It is a week of flamenco, sherry, horses and carriages, with women in flamenco dresses and men in short jackets. Much of it takes place in private marquees (casetas) that belong to families, clubs and businesses and are open by invitation only, though some are public. Dress smartly if you want to fit in, and be prepared for very high accommodation prices in Seville that week.

San Fermín, Pamplona (6–14 July): nine days of festivities starting at noon on 6 July with the firing of a rocket (the chupinazo), famous for the running of the bulls (encierro), which takes place at 8 a.m. each morning in the old town. It is dangerous and people are injured every year; runners are expected to be adults and sober, and strict rules apply, so read the city council's official guidance before even considering it. Many visitors simply watch from a safe spot, or skip the bull run and enjoy the rest of the week: the parades, the music and the street parties, in a sea of white clothes and red scarves. Pamplona's accommodation sells out and prices spike, so many travellers stay in nearby towns or San Sebastián.

La Tomatina, Buñol (last Wednesday of August): a one-hour tomato fight in the Valencian town of Buñol, held on the last Wednesday of August since 1945, as part of a week of festivities. Participants now need to buy a ticket in advance, so don't just turn up. In 2027 it falls on 25 August. Wear old clothes and shoes that you can afford to lose, protect your eyes, and leave phones and cameras behind or in a waterproof pouch. Squash the tomatoes before you throw them. Buñol is a small town, so many visitors base themselves in Valencia and travel in for the day.

THE REST OF THE CALENDAR

Carnival (February or March): costumed street parties across Spain. The best known are in Cádiz (satirical song groups and a very local sense of humour), Santa Cruz de Tenerife and Sitges. The date moves with Easter: in 2027 Shrove Tuesday is 9 February.

Noche de San Juan (23–24 June): midsummer bonfires on beaches across the country, with jumping over the flames, swimming at midnight and a lot of sand in your drinks. It's a national event and a good excuse to be on the coast.

Batalla del Vino, Haro (29 June): the "Battle of Wine" in La Rioja, where thousands of people dressed in white and red neckerchiefs soak each other in red wine after a morning mass.

Madrid: San Isidro, the city's patron saint's festival, is on 15 May, with picnics and traditional dress in the parks. Madrid Pride is one of Europe's biggest, around late June and early July.

Córdoba's Patios (early-to-mid May): the courtyard festival, when private patios full of flowers are opened to the public.

La Mercè, Barcelona (around 24 September): the city's biggest street festival, with giant puppets, human towers (castells), fire-runs and free concerts.

Semana Grande, San Sebastián and Bilbao (mid-August): a week of fireworks, concerts and street life in the two Basque cities.

New Year and Epiphany: on New Year's Eve (Nochevieja), Spaniards eat twelve grapes, one for each chime of midnight, and Madrid's Puerta del Sol is the national focal point. On 5 January, the Three Kings parades (Cabalgata de Reyes) fill the streets of towns across the country with sweets thrown from floats, as presents are traditionally given on 6 January.

And the thousands you've never heard of: almost every town and village has a patron-saint fiesta, usually with a funfair, live bands, fireworks, communal meals and dancing until dawn. They are often the most memorable events of a trip. Ask in your hostel, look for posters, and check the town hall's website (ayuntamiento) when you arrive. National holidays and local feast days also shut shops and transport (see Etiquette).

MUSIC FESTIVALS

Spain's festival season runs from late May to August, and July is the busiest month. Here are the major ones, with the dates for 2026 as a guide to the usual timing, and the 2027 dates where they have been announced. Dates move around by a few days each year, so always check the official site.

Primavera Sound, Barcelona (early June): one of the largest and most internationally known festivals in Europe, across a huge range of indie, pop, electronic and hip-hop, at the seafront Parc del Fòrum. 2027: 2–6 June, with a free opening day on Wednesday 2 June; tickets are on sale from October 2026.

Sónar, Barcelona (June): electronic music and digital art, held in central Barcelona venues by day and bigger spaces by night.

Resurrection Fest, Viveiro, Galicia (early July; 1–4 July in 2026): Spain's main metal, punk and hardcore festival.

Mad Cool, Madrid (July; 8–11 July in 2026): a big urban festival with rock, pop and international headliners.

Bilbao BBK Live, Bilbao (July; 9–11 July in 2026): indie, pop, rock and electronic, on a hillside above the city, with shuttle buses from the centre. 2027: 8–10 July.

Arenal Sound, Burriana, Castellón (end of July to early August; 30 July–2 August in 2026): a young, beachside festival.

Sonorama Ribera, Aranda de Duero (early August; 5–9 August in 2026): the flagship festival of Spanish indie, set in a Ribera del Duero wine town, with free concerts in the town square.

Rototom Sunsplash (mid-August; 16–22 August in 2026): a reggae and global-sounds festival.

Others: Barcelona Rock Fest (early July), Azkena Rock (classic rock, in the Basque Country), Granada Sound and plenty more. Check the line-ups and dates well before you plan your route.

IBIZA AND THE CLUB SCENE

Ibiza remains the world capital of clubbing, and the season has a pattern: a big opening weekend in late April (in 2026, 24–26 April for Pacha, Hï Ibiza, Ushuaïa and UNVRS, with DC10's Circoloco opening on 27 April and Amnesia on 9 May), a peak in July and August, and closing parties in late September and early October (in 2026, the main closings were on 2–4 October, with the last in the second week of October). The big clubs are Pacha, Amnesia, Hï Ibiza, Ushuaïa, UNVRS and DC10, and each has different resident nights and a different crowd, so check the line-ups before you buy.

Prices: tickets for headline nights run from around €70 to €120 at the opening events, and more for peak-season residencies; drinks inside cost a lot more again. Ibiza is expensive for a backpacker, and beds are at their priciest in July and August. Book early, and consider staying outside the main hubs.

The rules: in Sant Antoni de Portmany, pub crawls, party boats, happy hours and open bars are banned, and off-licence alcohol sales stop between 21:30 and 08:00. Commercially promoted villa parties are illegal, and you can be fined for attending. See the Drinking & Nightlife Laws and the Safety advice.

NIGHTLIFE BY CITY

Rather than a list of venues that change every year, here is where to look.

Barcelona: El Born, El Raval, Gràcia and Poble Sec for bars and live music; the seafront and Port Olímpic for the big tourist clubs. Take extra care of your belongings in El Raval and on the Ramblas at night.

Madrid: Malasaña (alternative bars and live music), Chueca (LGBTQ+ bars and clubs), La Latina (tapas bars, especially on Sundays), Huertas and Lavapiés for a mix of everything.

Valencia: El Carmen (the old town) and Ruzafa (bars, restaurants and a creative crowd).

Seville: Alameda de Hércules for a young, alternative crowd and Triana across the river for flamenco and tapas.

Granada: a tapas bar crawl in the city centre, where in many bars each drink comes with a free tapa, followed by a late night in the student bars.

San Sebastián and Bilbao: pintxo bars in San Sebastián's Parte Vieja and Bilbao's Casco Viejo.

Santiago de Compostela: the lively student bars on the Rúa do Franco.

FESTIVAL SURVIVAL GUIDE

Book early. Weekend passes (abonos) for the big music festivals often sell out months in advance and are released in price tiers, so the earliest buyers pay the least. Buy tickets only from the official site or its named partners. For fiestas like Fallas, San Fermín and Semana Santa, accommodation is the bottleneck: book it as soon as you know your dates.

Be prepared for the weather. Summer festivals are hot, sunny and dusty; bring sun cream, a hat, a refillable bottle and ear protection. Weather can also disrupt shows: the first full day of Primavera Sound 2026 was hit by severe weather, and several sets were cancelled.

Look after your things. Festivals are pickpocket territory. Carry one card, a little cash and your phone in a zipped pocket; leave your passport locked up; and agree a meeting point with your friends in case you lose each other.

Watch your drink and your friends. Spiking happens. Don't accept drinks from strangers, keep yours in sight, and look after anyone who seems more intoxicated than they should be. If someone is in trouble, call 112 and tell the medics what they took.

Get home. Check the last bus, train and shuttle times, and have a plan for getting back that doesn't involve an unlicensed taxi. Remember that your accommodation may have quiet hours or a late-check-in procedure.

Respect the fiesta. At religious events like Semana Santa, be quiet, don't cross a procession, and don't use flash. At secular ones, join in, but don't be the person everyone is stepping around at 3 p.m.

Digital Nomads

Spain is one of the best-set-up countries in Europe for remote workers: fast fibre internet in most cities, a sociable café-and-coworking culture, good transport, reasonable rents compared with northern Europe, and, since 2023, a visa made specifically for people like you. It is also a country where the rules — about visas, tax and renting — matter more than most travellers expect. This section covers the legal reality first, then where and how to live and work.

Read More

THE REALITY CHECK: TOURIST STATUS IS NOT A WORK PERMIT

Entering Spain as a tourist gives you up to 90 days in any 180 (shared across the whole Schengen area) and no right to work in Spain. Spain's legal position on a visitor who answers emails for a foreign employer on a short stay is not something this page can settle, and it's not legal advice; if you plan to work from Spain for more than a holiday, the safe route is the visa below. If you do stay as a visitor, keep the 90/180 rule in mind (see the pre-flight advice) and take legal advice if you're unsure.

THE DIGITAL NOMAD VISA

Spain's digital nomad visa (visado de teletrabajador de carácter internacional) was created under the 2022 Startups Law. It allows non-EU/EEA nationals to live in Spain while working remotely for companies, or freelancing for clients, outside Spain. The main points as they stand in 2026:

Income: you must show income of at least 200% of Spain's minimum wage. After the minimum wage was raised to €1,221 a month (paid in 14 instalments) for 2026, that works out at roughly €2,849 a month (about €34,188 a year) for a single applicant, with extra amounts required for family members who come with you (75% of the minimum wage for the first dependant and 25% for each additional one). Because it is pegged to the minimum wage, the figure changes when the minimum wage does.

Who you work for: your work must be for companies or clients based outside Spain. Freelancers may take on Spanish clients, but no more than 20% of your professional income may come from them.

Health insurance: you need private health insurance with full cover in Spain and no co-payments. Travel insurance doesn't count.

Paperwork: a clean criminal-record certificate, legalised or apostilled and translated, plus proof of your income and remote work. Start collecting documents early: some countries take weeks to issue a record certificate.

How long it lasts: if you apply at a Spanish consulate abroad, the first visa is typically for one year; if you apply from inside Spain while you're legally there, the first permit is typically for three years. Both are renewable and can count towards long-term residence. If your nationality needs a visa to enter Spain at all — South African passport holders do — you apply from abroad.

Check before you apply: thresholds, documents and procedures change, and different consulates are fussy about different things. Read the Spanish consulate's current instructions for your country and consider speaking to an immigration adviser.

TAX, BRIEFLY

Living in Spain can make you tax-resident there — generally if you spend more than 183 days in the country in a calendar year, or if your main base of economic interests is in Spain — which brings your worldwide income into the Spanish tax system. Visa holders can also be eligible for a special flat-rate regime (often called the "Beckham law"), which can be valuable and is complicated. Both are matters for a Spanish tax adviser, not a hostel noticeboard, so get advice before you settle.

WHERE TO BASE YOURSELF

Spain has three cities in the top ten of Holafly's 2026 global ranking of the best places for remote work: Valencia (third in the world), Las Palmas de Gran Canaria (eighth) and Seville (tenth).

Valencia: a big city on the Mediterranean, with a beach, a lively social scene, good public transport and cheaper rents than Barcelona or Madrid. A good all-rounder.

Las Palmas de Gran Canaria and Tenerife (the Canary Islands): mild weather all year round, which is the main attraction in northern winters, and a large remote-working community. A budget for one remote worker (rent, food, transport, utilities, coworking and leisure) is roughly €1,400 to €1,900 a month here.

Málaga: the Costa del Sol's tech hub, with a growing coworking scene, the Málaga TechPark, an airport with a lot of flights, and a beach. Costs are higher: roughly €1,700 to €2,400 a month for the same single-person budget.

Seville: culture, atmosphere and a good coworking network, but punishingly hot in July and August.

Madrid and Barcelona: the biggest ecosystems, with the most coworking spaces (Madrid has over 130), the strongest professional networks and the highest costs.

Smaller towns: cheaper and quieter, but fibre internet and coworking space are patchier, so check before you commit.

All of these budgets are rough estimates for 2026 from published cost-of-living guides, and your own spending will differ.

INTERNET, COWORKING AND KIT

Home internet: fibre is widely available in Spanish cities and typically costs roughly €25 to €50 a month, with speeds of 300 Mbps and above common. Before you sign a lease or book a month-long stay, ask the host to do a speed test and send you a screenshot, and don't rely on listing-site claims.

Coworking: a hot desk costs roughly €80 to €150 a month in smaller cities and €150 to €250 in Barcelona and Madrid. Some hostels have good work areas, and day passes at coworking spaces are a cheap way to try a city before you commit.

Backup: keep a data SIM or eSIM as a fallback, a power bank, and offline copies of what you need. The Iberian power cut of April 2025 took out mobile networks and card payments for hours (see the Safety advice).

Kit security: laptops and phones are what thieves target. Don't leave a laptop on a café table when you go to the toilet, don't work with a phone on the edge of a table, and use a lockable bag.

TIME ZONES

Mainland Spain is on Central European Time (UTC+1, UTC+2 in summer). That is one hour ahead of the UK all year, and one hour behind South Africa in winter and the same time as South Africa in summer. Spain is a comfortable overlap for Europe and Africa and awkward but doable for the US East Coast; the Canary Islands are an hour behind the mainland.

HOUSING

Short stays: hostels with private rooms, co-living spaces and short-term apartments are the easiest way to start. Some hostels and apartments will discount weekly and monthly stays if you ask directly.

Longer rentals: the rental market in the big cities and on the coast is tight. Expect to be asked for a deposit and often extra guarantees, to need a Spanish tax number for foreigners (NIE) for contracts, utilities and a bank account, and to register your address at the town hall (empadronamiento). Search on the main property portals (such as Idealista and Fotocasa) and be very sceptical of any landlord who asks you to pay a deposit before you've seen the flat, or who is "abroad" and will post you the keys.

FIT IN

Learn Spanish: it transforms the daily-life side of staying (see language schools). Adjust to the timetable: afternoons are slow, summer heat makes the hours before midday the productive ones, and August is quiet. Stay in a neighbourhood where people live, not only where tourists do, and respect noise rules: in some places there is local anger about the effect of short-term rentals and visitors on housing costs, and a nomad who becomes part of the neighbourhood is a welcome one.

The Camino de Santiago

The Camino de Santiago — the Way of St James — is a network of pilgrim routes that all end at the cathedral in Santiago de Compostela, in Galicia. Pilgrims have been walking it for over a thousand years, and today it attracts more people than ever: the Pilgrim's Office issued 530,919 Compostela certificates in 2025, a record. You don't need to be religious, fit or experienced. You need decent shoes, a light pack, a few weeks (or a few days) and a willingness to be a bit uncomfortable in good company. This section covers choosing a route, the paperwork, where you'll sleep, and how to stay in one piece.

CHOOSING YOUR CAMINO

There are many routes, and the right one depends on how long you have, the season, and whether you want company or quiet. Figures below are approximate, and the percentages are shares of the pilgrims who received a Compostela in 2025.

Camino Francés (about 780 km from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, France): the classic, and by far the busiest, used by 46% of pilgrims — 242,179 people in 2025. It crosses the Pyrenees, then Pamplona, Logroño, Burgos, León and the high plains of the Meseta, before climbing into Galicia. It has the most albergues, services and fellow pilgrims, which makes it the easiest to organise and the most crowded. Many walk only the final stretch from Sarria, just over 100 km out, which is the shortest distance that qualifies for a Compostela: 31% of all pilgrims start there, so it is very busy.

Camino Portugués (about 240 km from Porto, longer from Lisbon): the second most popular route and the fastest-growing, with a coastal variant. It is a good first Camino: gentle, scenic and well supplied.

Camino del Norte (about 825 km from Irún): follows Spain's northern coast, through San Sebastián, Bilbao and Santander, with spectacular scenery and lots of up and down. About 4% of pilgrims, so it's quieter, and it's wet at any time of year.

Camino Primitivo (about 320 km from Oviedo): the original route, a hard, mountainous walk through Asturias that is known for its views and tougher days. About 5% of pilgrims.

Camino Inglés (starting in Ferrol, just over the 100 km minimum): the short route to Santiago from the north coast, taken by about 6% of pilgrims. Starting from A Coruña is too short to qualify for a Compostela.

Vía de la Plata (about 1,000 km from Seville): the long southern route, running north through Extremadura (Mérida and Cáceres), Salamanca and Zamora. It is much quieter than the others, and it has been declining in numbers for a few years, so albergues can be far apart and there are long, hot stretches. It is not a first Camino, and it is a good one for a fit walker who wants solitude.

Finisterre and Muxía: a few more days' walk west of Santiago takes you to the Atlantic coast. Many people continue here after Santiago; it is not necessary for the certificate.

WHEN TO GO

Eighty-three per cent of pilgrims reach Santiago between May and October. Spring and autumn are the best compromise: mild enough to walk, and busy enough that everything is open. Summer brings the largest crowds and the worst heat, particularly on the Meseta of the Camino Francés and on the Vía de la Plata, where afternoon temperatures can be dangerous; start at first light and aim to finish walking by early afternoon. Many albergues close in winter, and the weather in Galicia and over the Pyrenees can be harsh, so winter walking needs more planning.

THE CREDENCIAL (PILGRIM PASSPORT)

The credencial is a folded card that you get stamped (sellos) at albergues, churches, bars and town halls along the way. It is your proof of the walk and your ticket to the pilgrim hostels, which require it. Get one before you start from an authorised pilgrim association in your country, from the Pilgrim's Office, or from the many places along the Camino that issue them. Only credenciales issued by the Cathedral or an authorised organisation are accepted for the Compostela.

Stamps: collect at least one a day; for the last 100 km on foot (or the last 200 km by bike), you need at least two stamps a day. Ask for one at every albergue and café you stop at; there is no such thing as too many.

THE COMPOSTELA

The rules: to get the Compostela you must walk (or ride on horseback) the last 100 km to Santiago, or cycle the last 200 km, with a stamped credencial. The service and the certificate are free. You must go in person to the Pilgrim's Reception Office at Rúa Carretas 33, Santiago de Compostela, a few minutes' walk from the cathedral.

Registering: the office uses an online registration system: you fill in your details beforehand, get a personal code, and show it at the door. Check the Pilgrim's Office website (oficinadelperegrino.com) for how it works now, and expect queues in summer.

Which certificate you get: the Compostela itself (in Latin) is for pilgrims whose reasons are religious or spiritual, or who describe an "attitude of search". If you walked for other reasons, you receive the free Certificate of Welcome. The Pilgrim's Office also sells a Certificate of Distance (€3), which records your starting point, route and kilometres.

ALBERGUES AND SLEEPING

Albergues are the pilgrim hostels along the route: dorm beds, shared bathrooms and kitchens in some. Municipal and parish (parroquial) albergues cost roughly €10 to €15 a night; some operate on a donation basis (donativo) and run on honesty and goodwill; private albergues cost more and usually take bookings. Many municipal and parish albergues don't accept reservations, so the bed goes to whoever arrives first.

The routine: albergues generally have a lights-out time of around 22:00 and want you out by around 08:00. Be quiet in the morning, pack outside the dorm, and don't leave your things around. You usually need your credencial and passport at check-in, and bring a sleeping bag or liner, earplugs and a head torch.

When it's busy — summer, and any stretch near Santiago, especially from Sarria — book private albergues or a pension a day or two ahead, and start walking early to reach the next town before the beds go. Pensions and cheap hotels are a good fallback.

Bed bugs: they happen. Check the mattress seams, keep your pack off the floor and bed, and wash and dry hot anything you suspect.

Eating: most towns have a menú del peregrino (pilgrim menu), a fixed-price meal with wine, and you can shop in small village grocers or supermarkets in the larger towns. Carry snacks and water on the long gaps, particularly on the Vía de la Plata and in the Meseta.

Camping: wild camping is generally prohibited in Spain, including along the Camino (see the Cycle Touring advice), so plan to use albergues or campsites.

WALKING WELL

Pace: most pilgrims walk around 20 to 25 km a day. Start slower than you think you need to, increase gradually, and take rest days when your body asks for one. The main reason people end their Camino early is injury from doing too much too soon.

Feet: shoes you've worn in, good socks, and attention to hot spots before they become blisters. Take your shoes off at every long break, and treat blisters as soon as you feel them.

Pack: a common rule of thumb is to carry no more than about a tenth of your body weight. Baggage-transfer services will move your pack from albergue to albergue for a fee if you need to lighten the load. Take only what you will use.

Way-marking: follow the yellow arrows and the scallop-shell signs. Download offline maps as a back-up, and don't rely on mobile signal in rural stretches.

Heat and water: the biggest practical danger. Walk early, drink regularly, wear a hat and sun cream, and know where the next water is. If you feel faint, sick or confused, stop in shade and get help.

Ticks: on the Vía de la Plata in particular, you'll be walking through rural western Spain, where Crimean–Congo fever cases have been reported in the Salamanca area in 2026; they are rare, but check your skin for ticks each evening (see the Medical advice).

Insurance: make sure your travel insurance covers walking and trekking, and carry your GHIC or EHIC if you have one. In an emergency call 112.

SAFETY AND SOLO PILGRIMS

The Camino is generally a friendly and safe environment, and many people walk it alone, including a lot of solo women. The same sensible precautions apply as anywhere: don't accept lifts from strangers, tell someone where you're staying, be careful about walking alone at dusk or on isolated stretches, and keep valuables out of sight. Dogs on rural paths are usually harmless but not always, so don't run from them. Stay out of the road verge where the route follows roads.

CYCLING THE CAMINO

You can ride rather than walk. The distance to qualify for the Compostela by bike is the last 200 km, and you still need two stamps a day for those kilometres. Walkers often get priority for albergue beds, bikes must be stored as the hostel directs, and the routes can be rougher than a road bike likes. See Cycle Touring for the rules on helmets, lights and trains.

ARRIVING IN SANTIAGO

Arrive, collect your certificate, and go to the cathedral. There is a daily pilgrims' Mass, and the square in front of the cathedral is always full of people who have just finished — hugging, crying and taking photographs. Santiago has an airport and rail and coach links for onward travel. See also Onward Travel for Portugal.

Where to read more: the official Pilgrim's Office website for the rules on the Compostela, and the long-running Camino de Santiago Forum, where pilgrims share route conditions and albergue news.

Surfing

Spain has thousands of kilometres of coastline on four very different seas — the cold Bay of Biscay, the Atlantic coast of Andalusia, the almost tideless Mediterranean and the volcanic Atlantic islands of the Canaries — and you can surf somewhere in the country in every month of the year. The north coast is the classic: powerful, consistent and cold, with a surf culture that goes back decades. The Canaries are warm and reliable. The Mediterranean is mostly flat. This section tells you where to go, when, and what to bring.

Read More

THE BIG PICTURE

North coast (Basque Country, Cantabria, Asturias, Galicia): the heart of Spanish surfing. It gets Atlantic swell from autumn to spring, and the best season is generally October to March or April. The water is cold — around 12 to 15°C in winter — so you need a thick wetsuit (a 4/3 or 5/4 mm is the norm). Summer is smaller, warmer and better for learning.

Atlantic Andalusia (Cádiz province): a long, sandy coast with consistent beach breaks, best in winter and spring, with warmer water and air than the north. This is also Spain's wind capital: Tarifa is a world centre for kitesurfing and windsurfing, with strong winds blowing for much of the year and the prime season from about May to October.

The Canary Islands: over 200 km of surfable coast with water at roughly 19 to 24°C, so a shorty or even boardshorts is often enough, and swell for much of the year. Many of the best-known waves break over reef or volcanic rock, which suits experienced surfers.

The Mediterranean: warm water, rarely surfable. Don't plan a surf trip around it.

WHERE TO SURF

Mundaka (Basque Country): a famous river-mouth left-hander, widely regarded as Spain's best-known wave and a former pro-tour venue. When it works, it is superb; it is also crowded and best left to intermediate and advanced surfers. The best season is roughly October to March.

Zarautz (Basque Country): a long beach with a more forgiving break, many surf schools, and a good option for all levels. Best between roughly October and April. It's an easy trip from San Sebastián, whose own city beach, Zurriola, is also a surf spot.

Sopelana and the Bizkaia coast: beach breaks close to Bilbao, reachable by metro. Meñakoz nearby is a big-wave spot for experts only.

Cantabria: a pioneer of Spanish surfing, with consistent breaks and a good place to learn. Somo, near Santander, is one of the best-known beaches in the country and the first surfing nature reserve in Spain.

Asturias: Rodiles (a left at a river mouth) and Salinas are two of the best-known spots, with a more rugged, less crowded feel.

Galicia: a long, rugged coast, nicknamed the "Coast of Waves" in the Ferrolterra–Rías Altas area, with plenty of swell, fewer crowds than the Basque Country, and plenty of rain.

El Palmar and the Cádiz coast: El Palmar is a long sandbar break that works for all levels, best on low-to-mid tide, and nearby Hierbabuena is another well-known Cádiz break. Both are best in winter and spring.

The Canaries: El Confital in Las Palmas (Gran Canaria) is described by the Spanish tourist board as the best wave in the Canaries; El Quemao on Lanzarote breaks over a volcanic rock bottom and is for advanced surfers only; El Socorro on Tenerife hosts competitions. Fuerteventura and Lanzarote are the classic surf-and-stay islands.

LEARNING TO SURF

The best places to learn are the beach breaks with surf schools and gentle waves: Zarautz and the other Basque beaches, Cantabria (especially Somo), the beaches of Cádiz, and the Canary Islands in summer. Surf schools offer lessons, board and wetsuit hire, and multi-day camps with accommodation. Look for a school that is registered, has qualified instructors, and keeps group sizes small. Prices vary by region and season, so ask at the school.

PRACTICAL NOTES

Gear: boards and wetsuits can be hired at most surf towns. If you are surfing in the north in winter, a good wetsuit with boots and, in the coldest months, gloves and a hood is not optional.

Flying with a board: airlines have their own rules and fees for sports equipment, and they can be steep on budget airlines. Check before you book, or consider hiring locally.

Hostels and surf houses: San Sebastián, Mundaka and other north-coast towns have hostels and surf camps that suit backpackers. Book early in July and August.

Getting around: the north coast surf towns are linked by trains and buses (Zarautz is close to San Sebastián and Sopelana close to Bilbao), but a hire car or a lift-share gives you access to the more remote breaks.

Etiquette: the usual rules apply. The surfer closest to the peak has right of way, don't drop in on other surfers, paddle around the break rather than through it, and be especially respectful at crowded, famous spots, where locals have strong feelings about visitors who cause trouble. If you are a beginner, stay out of advanced breaks.

HAZARDS

Rips and currents: beach breaks have strong rips, especially around river mouths and rocky headlands. Learn to recognise them, and swim parallel to the shore, not against the current, if you're caught in one. Follow lifeguard flags, and remember that a red flag means you must not enter the water.

Rocks and reef: many of Spain's best waves break over rocks, reef or volcanic rock, and wipe-outs there can cause serious cuts and injuries. Ask local surfers or the school about the bottom and the tide.

Sea life: jellyfish and sea urchins are common; wear reef boots on rocky coasts and ask locally how to treat a sting. See the Medical advice.

Cold: in winter, cold water and wind can lead to hypothermia faster than you expect. Don't go out too long, and warm up properly afterwards.

Cars: don't leave valuables in a car at a surf spot; break-ins at beach car parks are common. Take your keys and phone in a waterproof pouch or leave them with someone you trust. See the Safety advice.

Insurance: check that your travel insurance covers surfing and board hire (see the pre-flight advice).

Cycle Touring

Spain is superb for cycling: a huge range of terrain, thousands of kilometres of quiet rural roads, traffic-free greenways on old railway lines, mild weather for much of the year, and distances that are rarely too far between a bed and a decent lunch. It is also a country with firm rules for cyclists, hard summer heat, a restrictive attitude to camping, and trains that don't always want your bike. This section covers where to ride, what the law says, and how to cope with the practicalities.

Read More

WHERE TO RIDE

Vías Verdes (greenways): a network of more than 2,700 km of traffic-free routes on disused railway lines, ideal for relaxed touring, with gentle gradients and often tunnels and viaducts. One well-known example is the Carrilet route between Olot and Girona in Catalonia. They are the safest way to cycle in Spain, and good for beginners and families.

Quiet back roads: the backbone of cycle touring in Spain. Away from the main national roads, secondary and local roads carry little traffic, and links between villages can be delightful. Plan your route on secondary roads and use a navigation app that can avoid main roads (see the Cycling apps below).

EuroVelo routes: the long-distance European network includes EuroVelo 8 (the Mediterranean route), which crosses Spain, and EuroVelo 3 (the Pilgrims Route), which ends in Santiago de Compostela. Check EuroVelo's own website for the current state of the Spanish sections.

The Camino by bike: you can ride the Camino de Santiago and qualify for the Compostela if you cover the last 200 km, with two stamps a day on your credencial. See the Camino advice.

Cities: Madrid has a municipal e-bike system (BiciMAD) and riverside cycle routes at Madrid Río; Valencia has cycle routes along the old riverbed park; Barcelona has a growing network of cycle lanes. Cycling in the city centres is easiest outside rush hour.

Mountains: the Pyrenees, the Picos de Europa, the Sierra Nevada and the interior sierras offer serious climbing. Check the weather and the passes before you go, and remember that the temperature can fall dramatically as you climb.

THE RULES OF THE ROAD FOR CYCLISTS

Helmets: mandatory for cyclists on roads outside built-up areas, with limited exceptions (for example on long, steep climbs or in extreme heat), and mandatory for under-16s everywhere. In towns, adults aren't legally required to wear one, but you should. Fines for not wearing one outside urban areas have been reported at around €200.

Lights and visibility: a front and rear light are compulsory at night, at dusk and dawn and in poor visibility, and outside towns at night you also need reflective clothing or a high-visibility vest.

Other rules: no headphones while riding; the alcohol limit that applies to drivers (0.5 g/l) also applies to cyclists; only one person per bike (apart from a small child in an approved seat); and you must obey traffic lights and signs just like any other road user. Cycling on pavements is fined in many places. Cycle lanes are strongly recommended and sometimes mandatory where signed.

Motorways: cycling on motorways (autopistas) and expressways (autovías) is generally prohibited. Plan routes that avoid them, and don't take a wrong turn onto the slip road.

Overtaking: drivers must leave at least 1.5 m when overtaking a cyclist. It doesn't always happen; stay visible and don't ride in the gutter.

BIKES ON TRAINS, COACHES AND PLANES

Regional trains: Renfe's local (Cercanías) and medium-distance (Media Distancia) trains generally take bikes, usually free, though the number of spaces is limited and on some medium-distance services you may need a bike ticket (and, if you change trains, possibly one for each leg). Look for the bike symbol on the doors.

High-speed and long-distance trains: bikes are generally not allowed unless they are folded or dismantled and packed in a bag, so check Renfe's current rules before you rely on it, and check the separate rules of the other operators (Ouigo and Iryo). Don't assume you can roll a loaded touring bike onto an AVE.

Coaches: many coach companies want the bike packed, with the front wheel off and the sharp parts wrapped. Ask the company before you book.

Flights: airlines each charge their own fee for bikes and have their own packing rules. For a tour with a long internal leg, a one-way bike box or bag from a bike shop can save a lot of trouble.

Shipping: parcel services such as Correos can deliver a bike box within Spain, and some cyclists report it arriving in about two days. Ask your hostel or hotel if they will receive it.

CAMPING AND WILD CAMPING

Wild camping is generally forbidden in Spain. It is strictly prohibited in national and natural parks and on or near beaches and the coast (national law is usually cited as 200 m from the high-tide line), and regions set their own rules and fines, which range from tens of euros to thousands. Campfires are banned in natural areas because of wildfire risk, and causing a fire, even unintentionally, is a criminal offence.

In practice, a discreet overnight bivouac (vivac) outside protected areas, with no fire, no mess and an early departure, is sometimes tolerated, but it isn't a right and you can be moved on or fined. Never camp on private land without permission, near a campsite, or on a beach.

The legal alternatives: campsites (campings) are plentiful, particularly on the coasts and near towns; hostels, hostales, rural guesthouses (casas rurales) and, along the Camino, the pilgrim albergues all take cyclists. Always book or check ahead in July and August and during fiestas.

WATER, FOOD AND SUPPLIES

Water: carry more than you think you'll need, particularly in summer and in the empty interior. Villages usually have a public fountain (fuente): look for a sign saying agua potable (drinkable) or no potable (not drinkable). Don't drink from streams without treating the water. Tap water is safe in most of mainland Spain.

Food: small villages may have one bar, one shop, or none, and many shops close in the afternoon and on Sundays. Buy lunch and tomorrow's food before you leave a larger town, and plan around the opening hours (see the Etiquette and Food & Drink advice). A bar's menú del día is the best fuel stop in the day.

HEAT, WIND AND WEATHER

Summer: in the interior and south, ride at dawn and in the evening and rest in the afternoon. Heat can be dangerous: carry enough water, wear sun protection, and don't ignore dizziness or nausea. Check AEMET's warnings.

Wind: strong winds are a feature of Spain's southern tip and parts of the interior, and a headwind can be tiring. Check the forecast.

Seasons: spring and autumn are the best times for touring. Winter is cold on the high plateau and snowy in the mountains. Autumn storms can cause flash floods (see the Safety advice).

BIKE SHOPS, REPAIRS AND SECURITY

Shops: bike shops (tiendas de bicicletas) are found in most towns and cities, and there are large chain stores in the bigger ones, but a rural area may not have one for a long way. Carry spare inner tubes, a patch kit, a multitool, a pump, a spare cable and brake pads, and know how to fix a puncture.

Theft: bike theft is a risk in Spanish cities. Use a strong lock, lock the frame and a wheel to something fixed, never leave a loaded bike unattended, and ask your hostel where it can be stored securely. Take your bags and your GPS with you.

Insurance: check that your travel insurance covers cycle touring and your bike, and whether there are limits on value or on theft from outside a locked building.

APPS AND RESOURCES

Wikiloc and Komoot for GPS routes; offline maps for areas with no signal; AEMET for weather warnings; and the EuroVelo and Vías Verdes websites for route information. Download routes and maps before you set off.

Language Schools

Spanish is spoken by hundreds of millions of people, and Spain is one of the best places in the world to learn it: schools in every major city, courses that start every week, and a country where your homework is ordering lunch. A week of lessons at the start of a trip will transform the rest of it. A month or more will change how you travel — and, for some, how you live. This section covers how the schools work, what they cost, and what the visa rules mean for a longer stay.

Read More

HOW SPANISH COURSES WORK

Intensive courses: the standard format is about 20 lessons a week (four a day, Monday to Friday) in small groups, typically with a maximum of nine or ten students. Many schools also offer "super-intensive" courses of around 30 lessons a week, one-to-one lessons, evening classes, conversation and grammar courses, business Spanish, and preparation for the official exams.

Start dates and length: courses can start every week, so you can book anything from a single week to several months. The longer you stay, the cheaper the weekly price usually is. A four-week intensive course, combined with real immersion, can be enough to move up a level.

Levels: schools use the European framework (A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, C2), and you'll normally be placed in a class after a short test on your first morning. Be honest about your level; a class that is too hard or too easy wastes money.

Official certification: the DELE diplomas, issued by the Instituto Cervantes, and the online SIELE test certify your level of Spanish, and are useful if you need proof of Spanish for work or study. Many schools offer exam-preparation courses.

CHOOSING A SCHOOL

Accreditation: the school should be accredited by the Instituto Cervantes. This is a quality mark, and for stays of more than 90 days it is a legal requirement: only a school with this accreditation can issue the letter you need for a student visa. Look for membership of FEDELE, the federation of Spanish-as-a-foreign-language schools.

The city matters: classic study destinations include Salamanca (a university city), Granada, Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia, Málaga and Seville. Pick on the basis of what you want from the rest of your time: Madrid and Salamanca for immersion in everyday Spanish; Barcelona and Valencia for the beach and city life (where Catalan or Valencian is also heard); the south for the weather and an Andalusian accent. A smaller city usually means less English around you and more forced practice.

Class size and teaching: ask about the maximum class size, how placement works, whether teachers are native speakers with a teaching qualification, and how many hours of lessons are included.

The extras: many schools advertise "free" cultural activities such as museum visits and tapas evenings. Treat them as a bonus, not a reason to choose a school.

WHAT IT COSTS

These are rough 2026 ranges from published school price lists, and they vary by city, school and season.

Tuition: a standard intensive course (20 lessons a week) costs roughly €165 to €200 a week at the more economical accredited schools, falling a little for longer bookings, and roughly €200 to €350 a week at others. Exam-preparation, one-to-one and business courses cost more.

Accommodation: schools arrange homestays (a room with a Spanish host family, often with meals) and shared student flats. Expect roughly €160 to €350 a week, depending on the city and the type. You can also book your own hostel or flat, which is often cheaper for a long stay, though a homestay gives you more speaking practice.

Other costs: registration or materials fees, exam fees, and the cost of the student visa if you need one. Ask for the total price in writing.

VISAS: SHORT COURSES AND LONG ONES

Up to 90 days: if your nationality is visa-exempt for Spain, you can study on a short course as a tourist, with no student visa, within the usual 90 days in any 180 across the Schengen area. If you need a Schengen visa to enter Spain (South African passport holders do), you apply for a short-stay visa and the school provides an acceptance letter.

More than 90 days: you need a student visa. The school must be accredited by the Instituto Cervantes, and the course must be at least 20 hours a week of in-person teaching. Courses of 91 to 180 days fall under a short-term student visa, and courses of 181 days or more under a long-term student visa.

Apply from home: the rules were tightened in 2025: you can no longer expect to convert tourist status into a student status once you're in Spain, and applying from inside Spain has become risky. Apply at the Spanish consulate in your own country, and allow plenty of time (some schools recommend applying at least a couple of months before the course starts). Rules and documents vary by consulate and change often, so read the current instructions on your consulate's website.

Working: a student visa for a language course doesn't give you an automatic right to work. Don't plan to fund your stay with a job you haven't got permission to do.

TEACHING ENGLISH

Many backpackers think about teaching English in Spain. The legal position is the same as for any work: tourists can't work, and non-EU nationals need the right visa and permit. If it's something you'd like to do, look at accredited TEFL qualifications, and look at the official language-assistant programme run by Spain's Ministry of Education (auxiliares de conversación), which places native speakers in schools: check its website for eligibility and deadlines. Be wary of any school that offers cash-in-hand work with no paperwork.

REGIONAL LANGUAGES

If you're drawn to Catalonia, the Basque Country or Galicia, courses in Catalan, Basque and Galician exist too, often run by regional language institutions and local schools. They're an excellent way into the culture, but they are not necessary to get by, since everyone also speaks Spanish. See the Language advice for the basics.

MAKING THE MOST OF IT

Don't live in English: the biggest mistake at a Spanish language school is spending the evenings with other English speakers. Choose a homestay, make friends with locals and other learners who don't share your language, and attend the school's conversation exchange (intercambio) nights.

Use it every day: order in Spanish, ask for directions, read menus and signs, and write down every new word you hear. Spanish is spelt as it sounds, so reading aloud helps.

Plan the money: course fees are usually paid in advance and refund terms differ, so read the cancellation policy and check that your travel insurance covers cancellation.

Combine it with the trip: a week or two of school at the start of a long journey, in a city like Granada, Seville or Salamanca, gives you the phrases you need and a group of friends to travel on with. See also the Language section of the in-country advice.
